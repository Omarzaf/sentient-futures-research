Protein consumption and alternative proteins in India and Pakistan Muhammad Umar Zafar Evidence edition dated 13 October 2026 AI-assisted research draft. Human scholarly review is pending. This report brings together the comparative brief, food and nutrition data, consumer-study extraction, supply benchmark and cultivated-chicken work. It distinguishes source observations, analyst calculations, documentary interpretation and unresolved questions. The accompanying edition presents the same evidence in a shorter visual sequence. The comparison is informative but an adoption baseline is still missing India and Pakistan differ in the food available nationally, the quantities recorded by household surveys and the institutions relevant to new food products. These differences identify questions for a comparative study. They do not establish which country will adopt alternative proteins faster, how many consumers will buy them, or whether purchases will displace animal foods. The principal findings are: Pakistan’s poultry availability was 8.72 kg per person per year, compared with 3.52 in India, in 2023. These are FAOSTAT food-balance quantities available for human consumption. India’s pulse availability was 15.39 kg per person per year, compared with 6.53 in Pakistan in the same year. Familiar plant foods therefore belong in a study of competing meals; pulse supply does not measure demand for a manufactured meat analogue. The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World (SOFI) estimates that 40.4 percent of India’s population and 60.3 percent of Pakistan’s could not afford a healthy diet in 2024. Economic access is a separate outcome from measured dietary intake. The selected Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates (JME) country model estimates under-five stunting at 32.9 percent in India and 33.6 percent in Pakistan for 2024. These modelled estimates cannot be added to unaffordability or undernourishment counts. India’s official household-based protein estimates can be compared with adult requirement scenarios only after retaining the published consumer-unit denominator. The consumer unit is based on energy requirements, so this comparison cannot establish protein adequacy or population deficiency. Pakistan’s previously displayed complete protein total is withdrawn. Named physical conversions now describe a partial food basket, with unresolved foods and denominator reconciliation left visible. No national adequacy estimate or rural and urban model ranking survives that repair. The supply benchmark predicts national protein availability under exploratory models. Its held-out interval results differ sharply between the country series, and its forecast intervals do not supply probabilities of alternative-protein adoption. The consumer-study register contains 9 studies and 29 extracted findings. These are heterogeneous published results, not respondent microdata or a bilateral acceptance estimate. India has documented research support and food-regulatory instruments; Pakistan has halal institutions and a Punjab instrument containing a general novel-food provision. Neither a programme nor a rule establishes approval or sales of a named cultivated product. The halal work identifies product, process and institutional conditions. A religious position, a halal certificate and food authorization must each be established for the case being assessed. The main unresolved empirical requirement is a compatible observation of consumer behaviour for a specified product, place and period. The current record does not provide that baseline. The most useful next work is to obtain eligible respondent data, verify actual product availability and test questions about price, meal use and repeat purchase before making an adoption forecast. A common meal makes the comparative question testable The India–Pakistan brief asks when a new protein product earns a recurring place in a household meal. It distinguishes willingness to try from purchase, habitual use and the food displaced. That distinction remains the report’s organizing question. National food supply describes the context in which people choose; it does not observe their response to a new product. The brief is a secondary synthesis, with no project consumer experiment or interview evidence. The primary proposed comparison is a plant-based chicken product, a conventional chicken option and a familiar pulse-based meal. The options need specified serving sizes, prices, preparation demands and product descriptions. A cultivated-meat vignette would be analysed separately because its animal origin and production process introduce different questions. Precision-fermented ingredients and fungal biomass also require their own descriptions. A response to one category cannot stand for acceptance of all alternative proteins. The brief’s argument has an economic rival: price, taste and availability may account for differences that appear cultural. Comparable purchase conditions might reveal that differences within a country exceed those between selected groups in the two countries. Religious affiliation, urban residence or an aggregate food quantity cannot settle that question. The India–Pakistan research brief proposes a comparison that can distinguish these rival explanations. The foundational plan established an India–Pakistan research lane alongside support for forecast-question design. Its early policy-attention hypothesis was a proposal to investigate, not an empirical result. The later halal work expands the documented scope without cancelling the original comparative question. This report connects the workstreams through product-specific evidence while retaining their different methods. A forecast question needs a named product category, geography, outcome, denominator, deadline and resolution source. Retail availability, repeated purchasing and animal-food displacement are different events. Where no compatible baseline or resolution source exists, the question remains a monitoring or data-acquisition task. Repeated model answers do not supply the missing observation, and disagreement between language models is not a calibrated probability interval. The drivers are hypotheses about decisions Affordability concerns what a household can buy relative to familiar meals. The relevant comparison is a delivered serving at an actual outlet, including preparation and access costs where measured. A laboratory cost estimate or premium retail offer cannot establish a typical household’s choice. The household expenditure gradients in this report motivate price-sensitive questions without identifying an elasticity for alternative proteins. Dietary practice concerns ingredients, meal roles, preparation and the people who buy and cook. A product resembling meat may appeal to someone seeking a substitute and repel someone avoiding meat-like foods. Both possibilities fit the present evidence. A questionnaire should therefore ask what the proposed product would replace and whether it fits an ordinary meal, rather than treating resemblance as a universal advantage. Social legitimacy concerns whose approval matters and how a choice is understood in the household or community. The report’s culture matrix contains descriptive context, not a causal ranking of religions or nationalities. Region, income, caste, gender, access and dietary practice can overlap. Their independent contributions require a design that measures those relationships instead of assigning them from a national label. Certification concerns the credibility and scope of an assurance for a named product. A consumer’s belief, a religious authority’s conditions and a certifier’s decision are separate observations. For cultivated meat, donor history and culture inputs may matter as much as the final label. A generic assertion that a product is animal-free cannot resolve questions about every earlier production stage. Public policy concerns permission to sell, research support, standards, disclosure and the institutions responsible for those decisions. The plausible connection to consumption is through products, prices, access or trust. The current documentary evidence records instruments and events; it does not identify their effects on purchases. A funded project remains distinct from a commercial product reaching a household. The proposed sequence from acceptability to trial, purchase and habit is an analytical framework. It is not an estimated causal model. Trial could replace meat, replace a familiar plant dish or add an occasional novelty. Repeated purchases and the displaced food would need to be observed before the study could assess substitution. FAOSTAT food balances Food balance sheets estimate food available for human consumption within a national accounting system. They do not measure an individual’s ingestion, household distribution or retail sales of alternative proteins. The series can compare recorded availability under the source’s definitions. It cannot identify who consumed the food, how much was wasted after supply entered households or which products competed at a particular meal. The pulse comparison matters because familiar plant foods are already part of ordinary food systems. Large pulse availability may be compatible with interest in a meat analogue, indifference to it or preference for a familiar dish. None of those responses follows from supply alone. A study that compares an analogue only with meat would miss a relevant existing option for some consumers. The OECD–FAO Agricultural Outlook uses a separate method and weight convention. Its poultry projection is shown apart from the historical food-balance series, with its vintage and retail-weight unit. Joining the projected value directly to the FAOSTAT endpoint would create an apparent change partly from incompatible definitions. The report treats the outlook as a conditional projection from its source rather than an extension of measured household consumption. The Indian benchmark is a scenario and the Pakistan total is missing India’s published consumer-unit estimates are the appropriate denominator for the report’s adult reference scenarios. A consumer unit expresses energy needs relative to a sedentary adult man. It is not a protein-equivalent person. Comparing a household group’s mean protein estimate with an adult requirement therefore supplies a descriptive scenario, with an explicit mismatch that prevents an adequacy conclusion. The all-India estimates are 66.6 g per consumer unit per day in rural areas and 69.9 in urban areas. HCES fieldwork ran from August 2023 through July 2024; these months describe the survey period, not an individual respondent’s observation interval. The nutrient table’s analytical base remains distinct from the survey’s overall household sample. They answer different questions about requirements and cannot be swapped merely to produce a larger apparent gap. The report also retains alternative body-weight assumptions. None of these comparisons observes the distribution of usual individual intake needed for an estimate of population inadequacy. At a reference weight of 60 kg, the EAR scenario uses 0.66 g per kg per day, or 39.60 g per day. The RDA sensitivity uses 0.83 g per kg per day, or 49.80 g per day. These calculations are tagged derived even though their requirement coefficients come from reference sources. They are adult scenarios without a protein-equivalent household denominator. A group mean above a reference can coexist with individuals below it. A group mean below a reference does not tell how many individuals are below it. The next data collection should answer a named question The first task is to establish whether the identified respondent-data routes contain reusable observations with compatible product and outcome definitions. Term Meaning here SOFI The State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World: the interagency report series supplying the affordability estimates. HCES Household Consumption Expenditure Survey: the Indian household survey used for published nutrient estimates. MPCE and expenditure fractiles Monthly per-capita consumption expenditure.
