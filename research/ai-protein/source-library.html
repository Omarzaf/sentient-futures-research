13Scale-up economics for cultured meatDavid Humbird · 2021-06-07techno-economic/process-engineering model · full text via Wiley HTML
Finding
The analysis argues that animal-cell culture faces hard scale limits from slow growth, metabolic inefficiency, catabolite inhibition, shear sensitivity, sterility costs, and expensive amino-acid/growth-factor media. It concludes that low-cost plant hydrolysate media and large metabolic efficiency gains are necessary but still insufficient for broad meat displacement.
Limits
Conceptual facilities; relies heavily on CHO/biopharma analogies because public cultivated-meat production data were scarce; unstructured cell mass rather than mature consumer products.
Policy use
Cultivated meat forecasts should require public milestone evidence for media cost, cell density, contamination control, and bioreactor productivity before assuming commodity displacement.

Funding/conflict statement not found in inspected page section; author affiliation is DWH Process Consulting.

14Environmental Impacts of Cultured Meat: A Cradle-to-Gate Life Cycle AssessmentDerrick Risner; Patrick Negulescu; Yoonbin Kim; Cuong Nguyen; Justin B. Siegel; Edward S. Spang · 2024-12-30 online; 2025-01-17 issuecradle-to-gate life-cycle assessment with scenario analysis · full text/abstract via ACS, PubMed, and PMC landing pages
Finding
Modeled near-term animal cell-based meat can have much higher environmental impacts than beef if production depends on highly refined growth media. The result turns on media processing assumptions and shows that environmental benefits require sustainable, low-refinement media and high-density proliferation.
Limits
Modeled LCA grounded in TEAs and engineering/database assumptions, not measurement from an operating commercial or pilot ACBM facility; near-term scenario, not an optimized mature industry; cradle-to-gate only; sensitive to media purification factor and TEA inputs.
Policy use
Public funding should treat growth-media supply chains as an environmental gating variable, not a technical detail; LCA claims should publish media assumptions.
Funding and interests
Inspected PubMed/PMC landing pages did not expose a conflict statement; affiliations include USDA AI Institute for Next Generation Food Systems.

Finding
This early LCA estimated large land, water, and GHG advantages over conventional European meat, assuming cyanobacteria hydrolysate as nutrient and energy source. It reported 78-96% lower GHGs, 99% lower land use, and 82-96% lower water use versus several conventional meats.
Limits
Highly speculative process assumptions; predates later TEA critiques and detailed media supply-chain concerns; energy use not uniformly lower than poultry.
Policy use
Optimistic LCAs are useful as possibility proofs but should not anchor forecasts unless their nutrient, energy, and scale assumptions are still credible.

Finding
A multi-information-source Bayesian optimization workflow explored serum-free media for C2C12 muscle cells, balancing growth and cost. It found media with more than 100% greater growth than control and another with 23% higher growth at 62.5% of control cost from limited experiments.

29Most plant-based meat alternative buyers also buy meat: an analysis of household demographics, habit formation, and buying behavior among meat alternative buyersZachary T. Neuhofer; Jayson L. Lusk · 2022-07-29peer-reviewed primary empirical study · open access full text
Finding
About 20% of households bought a plant-based meat alternative at least once, but only 12% bought more than once. Only 2.79% of households purchased plant-based meat alternatives without buying ground meat in the studied ground-meat/PBMA categories. 86% of plant-based meat alternative buyers also bought ground meat. After a household's first plant-based meat alternative purchase, ground meat consumption did not fall.
Limits
Covers ground/patty-like products and ground-meat co-purchasing rather than all meat or all alternative proteins. The panel period ends in 2020 and may not capture later price, quality, and availability changes. Household purchases do not prove consumption or food waste effects.
Policy use
Policy should measure net displacement using scanner, foodservice, and production data rather than treating alternative-protein sales as one-for-one animal-product reductions.
Study design
transaction-level household scanner panel aggregated weekly for 38,966 households
Setting
United States households in IRI scanner data, November 2018-November 2020

30Impact of plant-based meat alternatives on cattle inventories and greenhouse gas emissionsJayson L. Lusk; Dan Blaustein-Rejto; Saloni Shah; Glynn T. Tonsor · 2022-02-11peer-reviewed primary economic model · open access full text
Finding
A 10% plant-based meat price decline or demand increase was estimated to reduce U.S. cattle production by about 0.15%. The same shock was estimated to increase plant-based meat consumption substantially but produce small cattle-inventory effects. Producer welfare losses and consumer welfare gains were material but modest relative to market size. Global emissions reductions depended on trade and land-use assumptions.
Limits
Model outputs are sensitive to cross-price elasticities, cattle supply elasticity, and the share of cattle value attributable to ground beef. It models U.S. cattle impacts rather than poultry, pork, eggs, dairy, fish, or multispecies animal welfare. It does not prove future consumer preferences under improved products.
Policy use
Price parity is useful but insufficient; displacement policy needs target categories, market-share thresholds, elasticities, and production endpoints.
Study design
calibrated economic model using demand elasticities, cattle supply, beef trade, land-use and emissions parameters
Setting
United States beef and plant-based meat markets, with global trade effects
