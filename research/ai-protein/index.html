AI and the Protein Transition

Alternative proteins could reduce reliance on animal production, but the evidence does not support a single technological timetable or a universal rate of animal-product replacement. The useful policy question is conditional: under what circumstances does faster protein innovation lead to less animal production, and which public investments remain worthwhile when those circumstances fail to materialize? This review develops the evidence needed to answer that question through 2030, 2035 and 2046.

The central finding is that the transition involves several distinct steps. A better model or cell line can improve an experiment without lowering a factory’s costs. A lower production cost can leave retail prices unchanged. Sales can grow without replacing animal products, and reduced consumption need not translate immediately into fewer animals raised. These distinctions are visible across laboratory studies, engineering models and purchasing research. They should organize both the literature review and the eventual scenarios. 1 2 3 4

The proposed contribution is a public policy paper linking each step to an observable indicator, a responsible institution and an allocation decision. It would combine a bounded technical comparison with evidence on adoption and regional consequences. It would not require a confident forecast of the global alternative-protein market in 2046.

Scope and review method

This is a structured rapid scoping review, with a literature search cutoff of 11 September 2026. It covers cultivated meat, precision fermentation, microbial biomass and plant-based substitutes; AI as both a production technology and a forecasting tool; demand and displacement; animal outcomes; regulation; and policy under deep uncertainty. Cultivated meat and fermentation are the principal technical cases. Plant-based products and conventional animal agriculture provide essential comparisons. Food preservation, cold chains and food waste remain relevant system conditions, but this review does not attempt a separate preservation-technology survey.

Searches began with the project bibliography and followed citations into original studies, institutional repositories and official publications. Inclusion required relevance to a link in the transition mechanism or a concrete policy decision. Engineering models, original experiments, purchasing studies, official documents and research syntheses are labeled separately. Abstract-only access is identified in the source library; inaccessible supporting data remain unverified. Undated project pages are described as accessed by the cutoff, without invented publication dates.

The evidence base is selective and English-language dominant. It is not a systematic review, meta-analysis or complete census of the field. No exhaustive database export, duplicate screening by two human reviewers, or statistical risk-of-bias assessment has been completed. Studies with incompatible products, units, baselines or designs are compared rather than pooled. The source library records the inspected material and limitations so that a human reader can extend or contest the synthesis.

The ethical motivation is explicit: sentient animals’ lives and experiences deserve consideration. That commitment does not settle whether a particular protein technology, subsidy or forecast will help them. The empirical analysis must remain open to disappointing results, competing interventions and harms created by the transition itself.

Technologies and the appropriate comparison

Alternative protein is an umbrella term, not a common production process. The four pathways below should not share an undifferentiated cost curve or approval forecast. Fermentation, in particular, can mean producing microbial biomass as food or using microbes to manufacture a specific ingredient. Evidence for one process does not establish the performance of the other. 5 6 7

Pathway What must be demonstrated What should be measured
Plant-based formulations Acceptable meals and ingredients at an affordable delivered price Repeat purchase, sensory performance, nutrition, waste and actual replacement
Biomass fermentation Productive, safe microbial cultivation and useful food functionality Yield, substrate demand, energy, downstream processing and consumer use
Precision fermentation Efficient production and recovery of a specified ingredient Titer, rate, yield, purification cost, functionality, allergenicity and customer demand
Cultivated meat Reliable animal-cell growth, differentiation where needed and food-scale processing Cell performance, medium composition, contamination, utilization, finished product cost and regulatory compliance

The meaningful alternative is broader than continued consumption of an identical animal product. An intervention may compete with legumes, tofu, improved conventional plant foods, reduced food waste or welfare improvements in existing production. A policy paper should therefore compare a new technical pathway with a credible lower-cost alternative, not merely with an unchanged livestock sector.

Animal-free production also needs its own evidence. Serum-free cell culture does not automatically mean every input is animal-component-free. Hybrid products may retain animal ingredients. Any claim about slaughter avoidance should specify the cell source, medium, scaffolding, formulation and continuing input requirements. 8 9 10

Technical feasibility and environmental disagreement

Cultivated meat economics

Humbird’s scale-up analysis is a necessary skeptical anchor. It models how biological performance, medium inputs and process engineering interact with operating and capital costs. It should be read as an explicit conditional engineering argument, not as a permanently fixed price for cultivated meat. Conversely, an improved medium or a favorable company announcement does not invalidate the model unless it changes its relevant assumptions at comparable scale. 11

Original cell-culture studies provide evidence of progress on particular constraints. Stout and colleagues demonstrate sustained bovine satellite-cell expansion in a serum-free formulation. Pasitka and colleagues report stable chicken cell lines and process development for serum-free production. These studies matter because they test biological mechanisms; their scope still differs from independent evidence of repeatable, profitable commodity production. 12 13

The policy implication is to distinguish funding for a promising scientific input from funding for a demonstrated production system. A proposed scale-up award should specify the performance that must survive larger batches, repeated runs and independent review. Useful disclosure includes medium costs at a stated purchasing volume, realized yields, downtime, contamination losses, product composition and facility utilization. These are proposed grant conditions, not an assertion that all firms already report them.

Why life cycle assessments disagree

The cultivated-meat LCA literature contains materially different systems. Early work by Tuomisto and colleagues and the anticipatory analysis by Mattick and colleagues establish how assumptions about inputs and energy affect prospective environmental comparisons. Later studies sharpen rather than remove those disagreements. 14 15

Sinke and colleagues model commercial-scale production in 2030, including an improved process and energy assumptions. Risner and colleagues analyze a nearer-term system in which medium ingredients and purification can carry large environmental burdens. Their results should not be compressed into a claim that cultivated meat is either inherently climate-saving or inherently worse than beef. They model different production conditions. 16 17

Comparison item Why it can change the result
Medium inputs and purification Chemical grade and processing requirements change upstream burdens
Electricity and heat A favorable clean-energy case is not the average grid everywhere
Biological and factory performance Density, yield, run length, losses and utilization determine inputs per output
Product and functional unit Wet biomass, finished food, edible mass and nutritional protein are not interchangeable
Comparator and system boundary Beef, chicken and plant products differ; cradle-to-gate studies omit later stages
Co-products and land response Allocation rules and land restoration assumptions affect claimed benefits

A defensible paper should reproduce a common assumption table before comparing headline results. Differences that cannot be harmonized should remain separate scenarios. No estimate here is a measured environmental footprint of the entire future cultivated-meat industry.

Fermentation and existing plant alternatives

Humpenöder and colleagues model replacing part of ruminant-meat protein with microbial protein and find substantial potential land-use benefits under their scenario. The replacement is an input to the model, not a demonstrated consumer transition. Sillman and colleagues’ power-to-food assessment similarly makes electricity and process efficiency central to the environmental result. Neither establishes that all precision-fermented ingredients have the same footprint. 18 19

Saerens and colleagues offer an original LCA of extruded burger substitutes. Crimarco and colleagues’ SWAP-MEAT randomized crossover trial provides product-specific health-marker evidence, with a small sample, short duration and industry funding. Neither justifies a universal environmental or nutritional claim for every product marketed as alternative protein. Nutrition, formulation and the displaced food should remain visible in policy appraisal. 20 21

AI as an intervention and as an incumbent advantage

Cosenza and colleagues provide directly relevant evidence for using Bayesian optimization to search for low-cost, high-growth culture media. Nikkhah and colleagues explore artificial-intelligence methods for reduced-serum media in a zebrafish-cell setting. These studies support investigating experimental efficiency; their species, objectives and retained inputs limit wider claims. They do not measure a reduction in commercial meat prices or animal production. 22 23

AlphaFold is useful context for the capabilities of computational biology. Its original structure-prediction results do not establish food-grade expression, manufacturing yield, flavor, safety or affordable protein production. A forecast should therefore treat an upstream AI breakthrough as a possible input to progress, with explicit additional steps before a market or welfare outcome follows. 24

The closest direct research synthesis is Moulange and McAuliffe’s 2026 report on AI and cultivated meat. It combines literature with five expert interviews and distinguishes current applications from possibilities. It supplies a valuable starting map of bottlenecks, but its limited search and sample do not establish a universal absence of private AI applications. The next useful contribution is to connect such bottlenecks to public decisions and evidence that would change them. 25

The conventional sector must also move in the scenarios. Ji and colleagues demonstrate machine-learning prediction within a robotic dairy setting. USDA ERS examines robotic milking, precision technologies and dairy profitability. Prediction accuracy, adoption and economic performance are distinct outcomes; neither source supports attributing every productivity gain to AI. Together, however, they make a stationary conventional-production comparator implausible. 26 27

The proposed causal test is comparative: does an AI-supported workflow improve validated performance relative to a suitable non-AI workflow, and does that improvement survive manufacturing constraints? Then ask whether the alternative improves relative to conventional production. Candidate measures include experiments required for a validated result, cost per usable output, batch reproducibility, held-out prediction error and transfer across laboratories. Investment in computational talent is justified only when evidence shows that computation, data or experimental design is a limiting constraint. It cannot substitute automatically for bioprocess engineers, operators or regulatory scientists.

Adoption and animal-product displacement

Household purchase data provide an important corrective to willingness-to-try surveys. Neuhofer and Lusk’s U.S. scanner study finds extensive co-purchasing of plant-based alternatives and animal meat, without a fall in ground-meat purchases after the first alternative purchase in the period studied. It does not establish that displacement is impossible, particularly as products, prices and consumers change. It shows why adoption should not be treated as one-for-one replacement. 28

Lee and Sumner’s 2026 fluid-milk study estimates substitution using a demand model. Its reported milk-displacement relationship belongs to the products, shopping data and static counterfactual analyzed. It is not a transferable coefficient for cultivated chicken, fermented egg protein or African food markets. Lusk and colleagues’ cattle model adds a further step: retail substitution interacts with animal supply, trade and the other products obtained from cattle. 29 30

Field experiments can evaluate policy mechanisms at the point of choice. Garnett and colleagues test cafeteria price changes; Ginn and Sparkman test plant-based defaults. Both concern actual selections rather than hypothetical preferences, but a selected or served dish does not reveal the diner’s entire diet or an upstream production response. Results should motivate context-specific trials rather than an assumption that the same effect size transfers to every public institution. 31 32

A practical accounting discipline

Separate four outcomes: additional alternative-product sales; reduced animal-product purchases; reduced animal production relative to a specified counterfactual; and changes in the number and experiences of animals. Each requires different data. A policy-attribution claim must use the difference caused by the intervention, not all sales in a growing category.

As a purely illustrative example, suppose animal production rises from 100 units today to 120 without an intervention. If an intervention lowers the future figure to 110, it prevents 10 units of additional production while leaving production above today’s level. This is a useful effect, but it is not an absolute decline. The OECD-FAO Outlook provides a dated conventional-market baseline; its existing technology and demand assumptions must be inspected before adding or subtracting another projection. 33

A cross-price elasticity is also not a displacement ratio. It measures the percentage change in demand associated with a percentage change in another product’s price. A volume replacement factor measures units of one product displaced per unit of another. Substituting one for the other without a demand model produces a false animal-impact estimate. The research should instead retain category-specific parameter ranges, specify units and show sensitivity to supply response and trade. 34 35

Animal welfare and regional distribution

Animal welfare is an outcome to measure alongside climate, nutrition and livelihoods. Original evidence such as Crook’s octopus study supports taking affective experience seriously without assigning identical capacities or numerical welfare weights to every species. Mood and colleagues’ estimates of farmed-fish numbers show why tonnage alone is an inadequate description of the animals affected. 36 37

Faunalytics’ updated Animal Product Impact Scales offer a useful accounting resource. Their product-level factors should be traced to the underlying data and assumptions before being used in a new marginal-impact calculation. A measure that already incorporates a demand or supply response must not have that adjustment applied again. In particular, lowering carbon intensity by shifting consumption between animal species can move individual-animal impacts in a different direction. 38

Regional equity belongs inside each scenario. FAO’s livestock-poverty analysis documents functions of livestock beyond marketed meat, including assets, livelihood security and farm-system services. It is a structural reference, not current evidence for the size of a particular country’s affected workforce. Each proposed country case needs recent national production, consumption, trade and livelihood evidence. 39

The suggested regional case should therefore begin with a named production system and stakeholder group. It should assess affordability and nutrition, electricity and infrastructure, access to finance, technology ownership, farmer and worker transitions, and who captures new value. A high-income retail coefficient cannot answer these questions. Nor should the presence of animal-dependent livelihoods be treated as an argument against all change. It is a reason to identify realistic alternatives and transition costs.

Policy instruments and existing policy research

Policy affects multiple parts of the mechanism. Public research can supply knowledge that firms underprovide; shared facilities can lower the cost of testing a process; regulators assess safety; purchasing institutions can test demand; and transition support can address losses borne by particular workers or producers. None is a universal substitute for the others.

CellAg’s 2026 German National Action Plan is particularly relevant prior work. The document reports a stakeholder questionnaire and interviews, then proposes action on authorization support, scale-up finance, agricultural integration and public trust. It is a research-and-advocacy contribution, not an adopted federal plan. Its priorities are a useful starting inventory for testing conditional allocation, rather than evidence that the recommended measures have already achieved their intended effects. 40

Reinhardt and Monaco’s original study triangulates legal analysis with stakeholder evidence on the EU Novel Food framework. The official EU framework, FDA’s first cultivated-food consultation notice and Singapore’s early approval record illustrate different legal stages and institutions. These historical documents are used to explain pathways, not to claim a current worldwide approval count. An FDA completed consultation must not be labeled a blanket product approval. 41 42 43 44

Proposed instrument Decision it could improve Evidence needed before expanding it
Open data and shared assays Whether promising R&D results transfer Independent reuse, comparability and validated improvement
Shared pilot facilities Whether a process survives scale-up Repeated runs, utilization, costs and failed as well as successful results
Regulatory support Whether applicants can produce adequate evidence efficiently Dossier completeness, review stages, safety performance and elapsed time
Procurement experiments Whether accessible products replace animal meals Purchases, substitutions elsewhere, repeat behavior, nutrition, waste and satisfaction
Worker and farmer transition support Whether change creates viable alternatives Local income, access, job quality and distribution of benefits

These are proposed evaluations, not an evidence-backed ranking of the marginal public dollar. Such a ranking would additionally require intervention costs, counterfactual additionality, administrative feasibility and comparable outcomes. Funding announcements should be separated from appropriations, awards, loans, disbursements and completed infrastructure. Cumulative public commitments cannot be compared directly with a single year of private investment to establish that one has replaced the other.

Forecasting and scenarios

Forecasts and scenarios perform different jobs. A forecast gives a testable estimate for a specified outcome; a scenario explores consequences under a coherent set of assumptions. IPCC terminology provides a useful distinction. The Foresight4Food guide supplies a participatory food-system process, and Haasnoot and colleagues provide a method for linking policy choices to changing conditions. Applying the latter to alternative proteins would be a methodological adaptation, not a proven forecasting advantage. 45 46 47

LLM forecasting research merits serious attention. Halawi and colleagues evaluate a structured system using retrieval and aggregation, while Schoenegger and colleagues study an LLM ensemble against a human crowd. These results concern particular systems, questions and evaluation settings. They do not validate a new panel of general chat products as a substitute for expert judgment on a twenty-year protein transition. Human crowd accuracy is also not interchangeable with the performance of selected superforecasters. 48 49 50

Repeated model outputs can reveal instability, but they do not create independent forecasting events. Different providers may still share data and reasoning patterns. A common evidence packet can introduce further correlation; discussion between models can add more. Work on diminished diversity of thought offers a reason to test, rather than assume, the benefits of an ensemble. 51

The proposed minimum standard has two layers. The scenario layer uses literature-grounded drivers and explicit challenges from people with different expertise. A small forecasting pilot uses narrowly resolvable questions, fixed source cutoffs, logged model versions, repeated runs, stated aggregation rules and separate reporting before and after deliberation. Probability scores and calibration require resolved outcomes. Historical tests with contemporary models remain vulnerable to training-data leakage even when the supplied documents are dated. No claim of long-horizon calibration should be made during a ten-week project.

Source quality and forecasting skill should be scored separately. A documented assumption can still lead to a poor forecast; an accurate guess can still have weak provenance. The range across model point estimates is a disagreement range, not automatically a prediction interval. If the pilot cannot support stronger interpretation, its useful output is a map of assumptions, contradictions and update conditions.

Proposed research agenda

The paper should test this question: Under what observable conditions would AI change the binding constraints on cultivated meat and fermentation, and how should public and philanthropic allocation respond to the resulting prospects for animal-product displacement? Germany within the EU is a useful initial policy case because relevant consultation work already exists. One contrasting regulatory example and one locally grounded regional case can test transferability without creating a global policy census.

An antithesis should remain live throughout: faster biological R&D may leave infrastructure, trust, demand and political constraints largely unchanged, while conventional producers use the same technologies. Existing plant foods and demand-side measures may then offer more immediate animal benefits than advanced biomanufacturing. The paper succeeds if it identifies when this alternative explanation is more persuasive, not only if it vindicates an AI-led transition.

The distinctive analytical object would be a policy decision table. Each row would connect a constraint, its best evidence, the affected product and place, a policy option, the required skills, an observable signal and a reason to revise the recommendation. It could extend technical bottleneck analysis and existing national roadmaps without claiming that either literature is empty.

Four proposed stress cases would test the table. They are exploratory cases, not mutually exclusive probability buckets or forecasts:

Stress case Condition explored Allocation question
Innovation reaches substitution Validated technical gains survive deployment and products replace animal purchases Which capacity and demand investments expand verified benefits?
Laboratory gains stall before markets R&D improves but scale-up, approval, finance or acceptance remains limiting Which specific institutional constraint is worth addressing, and when should support stop?
Transition proceeds with modest advanced AI Conventional plant foods, formulations and purchasing changes do most of the work Which near-term options deliver benefits without waiting for advanced biomanufacturing?
Incumbents retain the advantage Conventional production improves faster or new capacity fails to displace it How should resources shift toward other animal-welfare and demand interventions?

Every case should carry a regional distribution assessment rather than assigning equity to one exceptional scenario. Each should also distinguish chicken, fish, eggs, dairy and beef where the evidence permits. Production mass, edible protein and animals affected remain separate columns.

Proposed near-term signals include independent replication of an AI-related process gain; comparable pilot-scale cost and yield reporting; progress through named regulatory stages; repeat purchasing and measured animal-product replacement; and evidence that scarce occupational capacity delays delivery. The project should establish baselines and data access before setting numeric triggers. Any initial trigger is a decision rule chosen for review, not a scientific constant.

The literature leaves three particularly valuable empirical gaps: paired AI and non-AI evidence at relevant process scale; category-specific substitution connected to production response; and data showing which skills constrain deployment rather than merely appear in job advertisements. A bounded paper can make a useful contribution by defining and testing these interfaces, even without resolving all three.
