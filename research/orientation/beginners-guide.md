# Alt Protein Without the Jargon

The science, market, and policy basics behind alternative protein

> Earlier orientation draft; evidence cutoff 5 September 2026. AI-assisted; human verification pending. This is background reading, not the current project scope or a current market update.

PURPOSE  A practical mental model for entering the field. Read this before the full evidence briefing, then use the longer report to verify claims and follow sources.

## The five-minute version

CORE IDEA  Alternative protein is an attempt to produce the functions people obtain from meat, dairy, eggs, and seafood without raising and slaughtering an animal in the conventional way. Different technologies replace different parts of the animal-production process.

The field makes more sense when you separate product from production method. A burger is a product. It can be made from soy and pea protein, fungal biomass, precision-fermented fats, cultivated animal cells, or a mixture. “Alternative protein” is the umbrella; plant-based, biomass fermentation, precision fermentation, and cultivated meat are the main production tracks in this project. (GFI State of Industry reports, advocacy, Apr. 2026)

<tr><th>Stage</th><th>What happens</th><th>Who matters</th></tr>

The central technical mistake is to jump from R&D progress directly to market transition. Scale-up, finance, approval, manufacturing, distribution, and repeat purchase sit between them. A better model of change follows the whole chain. (GFI reports, advocacy, Apr. 2026; Rethink Priorities, original analysis, 9 Jun. 2026; inference)

## 1. The science

### The four tracks

<table>
<tr><th>Track</th><th>How it works in plain English</th><th>What the customer receives</th><th>Where it stands</th></tr>
<tr><td>Plant-based</td><td>Proteins, fats, flavours, and binders are extracted or formulated from plants, then structured through processes such as extrusion.</td><td>A finished food such as a burger, milk, egg substitute, or seafood analogue.</td><td>Commercial at global retail scale, with uneven growth and persistent taste, price, and repeat-purchase problems.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Biomass fermentation</td><td>Microorganisms or fungi are grown in vessels. The harvested microbial biomass itself becomes the main food material.</td><td>Protein-rich biomass used in products such as mycoprotein foods.</td><td>Commercially established in some forms. Economics depend on feedstock, vessels, processing, and product acceptance.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Precision fermentation</td><td>A microorganism is programmed to make a specific molecule. The molecule is separated and used as an ingredient.</td><td>A target protein, enzyme, fat, colour, or functional ingredient, such as a dairy protein without a cow.</td><td>Commercial products exist. Commodity-like food margins make fermentation and purification costs decisive.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Cultivated meat</td><td>Animal cells are selected, fed, multiplied, and differentiated in controlled vessels. The biomass is harvested and formulated into food.</td><td>Animal-cell material, sometimes combined with plant ingredients, fat, or scaffolds.</td><td>Regulatory and demonstration stage. Approval does not yet imply industrial-scale economics.</td></tr>
</table>

### Plant-based: food engineering first

A typical plant-based product begins with crops such as soy, pea, wheat, or fava bean. Processors concentrate or isolate the protein, combine it with oils, flavour systems, binders, colour, and water, and create structure through heat and shear. High-moisture extrusion can align proteins into fibrous textures. The scientific challenge is not merely protein content. The product must deliver texture, aroma, juiciness, nutrition, cooking behaviour, shelf life, and an acceptable ingredient list at a competitive price. (peer-reviewed plant-based review, 2025; GFI Plant-Based report, advocacy, Apr. 2026)

The market is mature enough that consumer evidence matters more than laboratory novelty. A technically impressive prototype can fail if people do not repurchase it. This is why falling unit sales in US plant-based meat can coexist with growth in broad global plant-based categories that include milk and other products. (GFI Plant-Based report, advocacy using Euromonitor and SPINS, Apr. 2026, p. 17)

### Fermentation: two different businesses

Biomass fermentation and precision fermentation are often collapsed, but their economics differ. In biomass fermentation, the organism is the output, so separation may be relatively simple. In precision fermentation, the organism is the factory and the target molecule must often be recovered and purified. The second route can produce highly functional ingredients, but downstream purification can dominate cost. (GFI Fermentation report, advocacy, Apr. 2026; peer-reviewed fermentation reviews, 2024)

Both depend on strain productivity, low-cost feedstock, contamination control, reliable large vessels, energy, and downstream processing. Scale creates physical problems that do not appear in a flask: mixing, heat removal, oxygen transfer, foam, cleaning, batch failure, and equipment utilization. This is industrial biotechnology under food-price constraints. (GFI Fermentation report, advocacy, Apr. 2026; peer-reviewed reviews, 2024)

### Cultivated meat: cell biology meets food manufacturing

The simplified sequence is: obtain and bank suitable animal cells; develop a stable cell line; formulate a serum-free growth medium; expand cells in bioreactors; induce differentiation where needed; create structure or combine the cells with other ingredients; harvest; and manufacture under food-safety controls. Every step affects yield, quality, cost, and regulatory evidence. (Annual Review of Animal Biosciences, peer-reviewed, 2024; FDA, official, 2026)

The main bottlenecks are not reducible to one media-price number. They include cell-line stability, media consumption per kilogram, oxygen and nutrient delivery, shear stress, contamination, vessel design, scaffolds for structured tissue, downstream recovery, food-grade asepsis, capital cost, utilization, and consistent sensory performance. Published techno-economic analyses disagree because they assume different processes and rates of technical progress. (CE Delft, GFI-commissioned analysis, 2021; Humbird, independent engineering preprint, 2021)

REMEMBER  Regulatory approval answers “may this product be sold under stated conditions?” It does not answer “can this be made cheaply, reliably, and at useful scale?”

## Where AI fits in the science

AI is best understood as a set of tools for choosing experiments, finding patterns in biological data, predicting candidates, and controlling processes. It can support cell-line screening, media formulation, strain and protein engineering, microscopy, ingredient discovery, sensory measurement, and bioprocess monitoring. These are live research or commercial practices in parts of the field, but the maturity varies sharply. (Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence, peer-reviewed review, 2024; Rethink Priorities, original analysis, 9 Jun. 2026)

<table>
<tr><th>Use</th><th>What AI might improve</th><th>What AI still needs</th></tr>
<tr><td>Cell lines</td><td>Classify phenotypes, select candidates, and prioritize experiments.</td><td>Labeled cell data, reproducible assays, wet-lab validation, and stable performance at scale.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Growth media</td><td>Search combinations more efficiently using active learning or Bayesian optimization.</td><td>Comparable recipes and outcomes, reliable assays, ingredient access, and validation in relevant vessels.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Strains and proteins</td><td>Predict structures or functions and screen sequences before laboratory work.</td><td>High-throughput assays, host biology, fermentation validation, and proof that the ingredient works in food.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Process control</td><td>Detect anomalies, infer hard-to-measure states, and optimize a running process.</td><td>Instrumented facilities, repeated production runs, process historians, and operators who trust the system.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Formulation and sensory</td><td>Measure texture or appearance and optimize formulations across many ingredients.</td><td>Human sensory data, consumer validation, and reproducible manufacturing.</td></tr>
</table>

The evidence does not show that AI has already moved the sector’s main constraint from capital to computational talent. Rethink Priorities found sparse realized adoption, scarce and proprietary data, limited sector-specific validation, and continuing infrastructure constraints. It also found no verified evidence that AI had already caused large media-cost reductions. (Rethink Priorities, original analysis based on literature and five expert interviews, 9 Jun. 2026)

The adversarial point matters: conventional animal agriculture can use the same advances in genetics, sensing, optimization, and automation. It has larger operating datasets and more installed infrastructure. AI changes the transition only if its relative benefit to alternatives exceeds the gains captured by incumbents or changes a regulatory, capital, or demand constraint. (Rethink Priorities, original analysis, 9 Jun. 2026; inference)

### Terms worth knowing

Cell line. A population of cells selected and maintained for repeated growth. Commercial usefulness requires stability, productivity, and documented provenance. (Annual Review, peer-reviewed, 2024)

Growth medium. The liquid mixture that supplies cells with nutrients and signals. Cost per litre is less informative than medium consumed per kilogram of usable output. (Rethink Priorities, 2026; inference)

Strain. A particular microorganism used in fermentation. Strain engineering changes what it produces and how efficiently. (GFI Fermentation report, Apr. 2026)

Bioreactor. A controlled vessel for growing cells or microorganisms. Large size changes mixing, heat, oxygen, and contamination risk. (peer-reviewed reviews, 2024)

Downstream processing. The recovery, separation, purification, concentration, or drying steps after biological production. (peer-reviewed fermentation reviews, 2024)

Techno-economic analysis. A model linking process assumptions to capital and operating costs. It is an engineered scenario, not an audited future price. (CE Delft and Humbird, 2021)

## 2. The market

MARKET REALITY  Only plant-based food has a large, measurable retail market. Precision and biomass fermentation have commercial products but weak category-level revenue disclosure. Cultivated meat has approvals and demonstrations, not transparent mass-market sales.

Global retail sales across plant-based meat, seafood, milk, yogurt, ice cream, cheese, and eggs were estimated at $28.9 billion in 2025, up 3 percent in current nominal dollars. Meat and seafood accounted for $6.6 billion. Europe was the largest broad region at $10.1 billion, followed by Asia-Pacific at $9.2 billion and North America at $6.9 billion. These are Euromonitor estimates reproduced by GFI, not audited producer revenue. (GFI Plant-Based State of Industry, advocacy using Euromonitor, Apr. 2026, p. 17)

The broad total can mislead. Plant-based milk has different consumer habits, pricing, and distribution from plant-based meat. Current-dollar growth can occur without unit growth. US plant-based meat and seafood continued to contract even while the wider global category grew. Any market claim needs a product, geography, time period, and unit of measure. (GFI Plant-Based report, advocacy using Euromonitor and SPINS, Apr. 2026)

### How value is created and lost

<table>
<tr><th>Layer</th><th>Examples</th><th>Typical failure question</th></tr>
<tr><td>Technology</td><td>Cell line, strain, recipe, extrusion process, media formulation.</td><td>Does it work outside a controlled demonstration?</td></tr>
<tr><td>Infrastructure</td><td>Fermenters, cell-culture vessels, purification, food plants, cold chain.</td><td>Can the facility run often enough and cheaply enough?</td></tr>
<tr><td>Regulation</td><td>Safety dossier, inspection, label, import and ingredient rules.</td><td>Does approval arrive before the company runs out of money?</td></tr>
<tr><td>Commercialization</td><td>Brand, distributor, food service, retailer, price, merchandising.</td><td>Will buyers list it and will consumers repurchase it?</td></tr>
<tr><td>Finance</td><td>R&amp;D grants, venture equity, debt, project finance, public loans.</td><td>Is the capital instrument matched to the risk and time horizon?</td></tr>
</table>

Leading firms are best treated as examples, not a stable ranking. Plant-based examples include Beyond Meat, Impossible Foods, and Heura. Precision-fermentation examples include Perfect Day, EVERY, Remilk, Imagindairy, Vivici, Onego Bio, and Formo. Biomass examples include Quorn, MyForest Foods, Prime Roots, and The Protein Brewery. Cultivated firms with regulatory milestones include UPSIDE Foods, GOOD Meat, Mission Barns, Wildtype, Vow, PARIMA, and Aleph Farms. (GFI reports, advocacy, Apr. 2026; FDA and Singapore SFA, official, 2026)

### Why companies have closed or consolidated

There is no single accepted explanation. The recurring mechanisms are expensive scale-up, weak or delayed demand, long regulatory timelines, high interest rates and reduced venture appetite, facilities built before economics were proven, and technical performance that did not improve quickly enough. Meatable failed to secure financing; Believer Meats ceased operations after building a large facility and receiving an FDA consultation outcome. Those cases show that approval and infrastructure can arrive without a viable financing path. (Meatable, company statement, 2025; Institute of Food Technologists, professional press, 23 Mar. 2026; GFI reports, Apr. 2026)

The headline “70+ companies shut down” is too strong. Green Queen’s tracker counted more than 70 companies acquired, merged, insolvent, or closed since September 2024. GFI separately identified at least 19 plant-based acquisitions during 2025 plus other pauses and closures. Acquisition, merger, insolvency, and cessation are different outcomes. (Green Queen, trade press, 16 Jun. 2026; GFI Plant-Based report, advocacy, Apr. 2026, pp. 10-12)

### The capital reset

GFI’s series estimates $1.6 billion of private investment in 2023, $1.1 billion in 2024, and $881 million in 2025. The last figure was published in a report titled 2026 State of the Industry because GFI changed its naming convention. “Below $1 billion in 2026” is a dating error; full-year 2026 was not available on 5 September 2026. (GFI reports and investment methodology, advocacy using Net Zero Insights, 2024-2026)

European private investment reached €236 million in the first half of 2026, up 56 percent year on year, but fermentation received €199 million and deal count fell. European public research and innovation funding separately fell in 2025. The correct reading is a concentrated half-year private rebound, not a general European funding boom. (GFI Europe, advocacy using Net Zero Insights, 27 Aug. 2026; GFI Europe R&I tracker, 2026)

## 3. The policy

Policy affects the field through at least five channels: product approval; labeling and sales rules; public research; scale-up finance and shared infrastructure; and demand-side action such as procurement. These instruments solve different problems. A research grant cannot finance a commercial plant, and a safety approval cannot create consumer demand. (GFI State of Global Policy, advocacy, Apr. 2026; regulator sources, 2026; inference)

### How approval works

<table>
<tr><th>Jurisdiction</th><th>Basic pathway</th><th>Status by 5 Sep. 2026</th></tr>
<tr><td>United States</td><td>FDA reviews cell collection and growth. USDA-FSIS regulates harvest onward for amenable livestock and poultry, including inspection and label approval. FDA alone covers most seafood.</td><td>Five FDA consultations were complete. State bans and constitutional challenges remained active.</td></tr>
<tr><td>European Union</td><td>Cultivated products require Novel Food authorization after EFSA safety assessment and Commission/member-state action.</td><td>Applications were under review; no cultivated product authorization was confirmed. Meat-term restrictions advanced in 2026.</td></tr>
<tr><td>United Kingdom</td><td>FSA and Food Standards Scotland use the regulated-products pathway.</td><td>No cultivated human food authorization. A £1.6m sandbox aims to improve the process; a 2024 approval concerned pet food.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Singapore</td><td>SFA applies a defined novel-food premarket framework.</td><td>Multiple product-specific approvals, including 2026 cultivated duck and beef milestones.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Israel</td><td>Ministry of Health safety review plus labeling and inspection conditions.</td><td>Aleph Farms received a 2024 “no questions” conclusion; remaining conditions matter.</td></tr>
<tr><td>China</td><td>National planning supports cultivated meat and industrial biotechnology; the product-approval route is still developing.</td><td>No cultivated-meat market authorization was confirmed.</td></tr>
</table>

Approval counts are easy to inflate because a safety consultation, formal authorization, facility inspection, label approval, and market launch are separate events. A clean regulatory tracker needs a product-level timeline for each stage. (FDA, USDA-FSIS, EFSA, UK FSA, and Singapore SFA, official, 2024-2026; inference)

### The live political fights

Seven US states had enacted cultivated-meat bans or moratoria by the end of 2025. In March 2026, the Eleventh Circuit declined to block Florida’s ban on the theory that federal meat and poultry statutes expressly preempt it. The broader conflict concerns state agricultural protection, consumer claims, interstate commerce, speech, and the boundary between federal inspection and state market exclusion. (GFI State of Global Policy, advocacy, Apr. 2026, p. 15; Eleventh Circuit, official opinion, 23 Mar. 2026)

Market. Plant-based has real retail scale; fermentation has products but weak category revenue disclosure; cultivated meat has approvals without mass-scale economics. (GFI reports, Apr. 2026)

Capital. The contraction is real, but the headline is 2025 investment reported in 2026. Public commitments are broader but do not replace private capital one for one. (GFI reports, Apr. 2026)

Policy. Approval, labeling, state bans, public R&D, loans, infrastructure, and procurement are different policy mechanisms. (official regulators and GFI policy report, 2026)

AI. AI is already used in narrow R&D workflows. Evidence for a sector-wide bottleneck shift is thin, and data plus infrastructure remain constraints. (Rethink Priorities, 9 Jun. 2026)

Foresight. A useful scenario changes a decision and has a near-term discriminator. A story with no signal or falsifier is not yet useful. (Foresight4Food guide, Jul. 2025; inference)

Scope. The project’s core can be a causal test of bottleneck change. Funding, talent, and policy then become conditional implications rather than three separate surveys. (project-scope inference, 5 Sep. 2026)
