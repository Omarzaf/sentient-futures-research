Protein Transitions Across Unequal Food Systems

Abstract

This paper examines protein consumption, the propensity to change protein sources, and public institutions that can help develop a protein-transition workforce across selected Global North and Global South settings. Its central analytical proposition is that protein diversification is a problem of matching nutritional needs, food practices, production capabilities and institutional capacity. An innovation policy aimed at maximizing new-product supply is insufficient if it does not establish who can afford those products, what they replace, and which capabilities remain locally available.

The comparison combines a reproducible extraction of country protein-supply data, government dietary-survey evidence, academic adoption studies and documented public research programmes. National food balances provide a common supply baseline; they do not measure individual intake. Consumer studies provide evidence about particular populations and products; hypothetical willingness does not establish repeat purchasing or animal-product displacement. Official funding and institutional records establish capabilities or commitments; they do not constitute a comparable census of employment. These distinctions govern the interpretation throughout. [1][2]

The resulting argument supports differentiated public portfolios: evaluate affordable traditional and novel protein options together; connect university education to practical production, safety and adoption problems; and fund international partnerships that leave durable capabilities in participating regions. AI-assisted research is a conditional part of this portfolio. Laboratory optimization is demonstrated in specific systems, but its translation into manufacturing economics, recruitment priorities and dietary change requires additional evidence. The proposed contribution to protein-transition foresight is therefore a set of conditional allocation decisions and observable revision rules, rather than a single prediction of technological substitution. [3][4]

1. Research question and argument

Under what nutritional, economic and institutional conditions can different protein sources achieve sustained adoption across contrasting national settings, and how should governments and universities build, attract and retain the talent needed for those transitions? This formulation makes demand and capability part of the same research problem. It also specifies a decision audience: ministries, public research funders, universities, technical colleges and international research partnerships.

The thesis to be tested is that the most useful public investment is the one that relieves a demonstrable constraint in a particular food system. That constraint might be cell biology, food texture, an approval dossier, reliable pilot equipment, distribution, cooking skills or purchasing power. Calling every constraint a shortage of scientists would misdirect training. Calling every adoption failure a communication problem would be equally premature. The comparison must therefore distinguish the existence of a useful technology from the conditions under which it improves diets or replaces incumbent production.

The central antithesis is that a protein-transition agenda organized around new technologies could absorb resources while contributing little to accessible nutrition or animal-product displacement. Traditional foods, existing food-processing capabilities, targeted nutrition programmes and improvements to incumbent systems might provide greater additional benefit. That proposition is not assumed to be true either. It defines the comparator against which novel-protein policies must earn their place. UNEP’s assessment considers benefits alongside environmental, health, social and welfare uncertainties; it does not justify a technology-independent endorsement of every alternative. [5]

A second antithesis concerns geography. The North–South distinction may be less explanatory than food security, income distribution, incumbent dietary patterns, industrial capabilities and public institutions. The study therefore uses the distinction as a way to expose unequal starting points, while retaining countries and population groups as the actual units of interpretation. It does not infer that a country shares an adoption pathway merely because it shares a geopolitical label.

Five questions structure the comparison: What protein is available, and what is actually consumed? Which substitutions are culturally acceptable and economically feasible? Where do observed purchases support or contradict stated intentions? Which institutions can convert research into safe, affordable food? Which talent interventions create capacity that would otherwise be absent? The final question introduces additionality: a programme that relocates an already available scientist is different from one that trains and retains an otherwise excluded cohort of technicians.

2. Comparative method and evidence boundaries

2.1 Case selection

The numerical baseline covers twelve countries with usable rows in one FAO publication: the United States, Germany, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, Denmark, China, India, Brazil, South Africa, Kenya, Nigeria and Zimbabwe. Singapore is included in the institutional comparison, but its blank row in the selected table is not filled with an estimate from another source. This is a purposive comparison, not a representative global sample. The institutional examples also extend beyond the countries with more developed consumer evidence.

For this paper’s organizing convention, the five selected North American and European cases are grouped as North; China, India, Brazil and the four African cases are grouped as South. These labels are neither latitude bands nor official statistical aggregates. Singapore is treated separately. No average for either group is calculated: such a statistic would depend on a defensible membership rule, population weights, complete coverage and treatment of missing data. The maps show the actual selected locations instead.

2.3 Eligible sources and review design

Empirical references are restricted to government publications, intergovernmental organizations and academic journal articles. Government research agencies and their award records qualify as government sources. A journal article retains its identity when an accessible copy is hosted in a public repository; a corporate blog does not become an academic source merely because it cites a journal. Industry and advocacy reports are not used as authority for the paper’s empirical conclusions.

The review is an iterative, targeted comparative evidence synthesis. It combines concept searches, country and pathway searches, citation follow-up, targeted searches for contradictory findings, and source-level checks. It is not represented as a preregistered systematic review, and no artificial screening count is supplied. The bibliography records access limits. A publisher abstract can support the explicitly stated abstract result, but cannot support an uninspected methods claim or an invented quality assessment.

Broader synthesis is triangulated where distinct evidence is available. Two publications reproducing the same dataset are not independent corroboration, and two official pages announcing the same grant do not prove two separate investments. A direct statistical observation is attributed to its data source; a general causal claim requires stronger support. Where comparable information is absent, the paper specifies an evidence gap rather than converting a lack of retrieved studies into a finding that no activity exists.

3. Protein supply and consumption across selected settings

3.1 A common numerical baseline

FAO’s 2025 yearbook reports 2023 total protein supply of 123.1 g/person/day for the United States, 109.2 for Germany, 131.0 for China, 106.5 for Brazil, 74.6 for India and 79.2 for South Africa. The corresponding animal-origin shares, calculated from the published totals, are 67.3%, 67.4%, 41.4%, 63.8%, 26.8% and 46.1%. These are country food-supply estimates. They already contradict a simple ordering in which every Northern country has more total protein than every Southern country. [9]

3.2 What this contrast means

The useful comparative question is not simply whether a society consumes more or less protein. It is whether a proposed intervention preserves or improves nutritional access while changing the source mix in a desirable direction. A high plant-origin share might reflect culinary preference, restricted access to animal foods, or several factors together. It cannot be interpreted as an already completed ethical or environmental transition. Equally, a high animal-origin share cannot identify the individuals who would benefit most from dietary change.

The country contrasts suggest different research starting points. In a high-supply setting, investigate substitution within existing food expenditure and diets. In a lower-supply setting, test affordability and nutritional access before treating willingness to try a premium product as a policy priority. In a setting with substantial plant protein already, compare the proposed novel product with familiar plant foods as well as with meat. These are hypotheses for programme design, not classifications of national character.

3.3 Supply is not dietary intake: India and the UK

India’s statistics ministry reports unadjusted 2023–24 household-based protein estimates of 61.8 g/person/day in rural areas and 63.4 in urban areas. Its adjusted estimates are 61.2 and 62.9 respectively. Cereals account for 45.9% of the reported rural protein mix and 38.7% of the urban mix; pulses account for 8.7% and 9.1%. These observations show why a “plant protein” category must extend beyond the foods marketed specifically as protein alternatives. [11]

The household estimates differ in concept and time period from FAO’s calendar-year supply. They cannot be used to calculate an apparent national “waste rate” by subtraction. Within the Indian source itself, adjusted and unadjusted estimates must also remain distinct. The release explains adjustments involving meals consumed by household and non-household members. [11]

The UK’s 2019–2023 survey uses individual dietary recalls rather than a national supply account. Its methods document substantial energy underreporting and explicitly state that the extent to which protein is misreported relative to other macronutrients is not known. A correction factor inferred from energy therefore should not be applied mechanically to protein. A harmonized international intake comparison would require age, sampling, measurement and reporting adjustments beyond this paper’s supply comparison. [2]

3.4 Nutrition constrains the transition objective

WHO’s healthy-diet guidance allows protein from both animal and plant sources and emphasizes dietary variety. FAO’s assessment of terrestrial animal-source foods addresses important nutrient contributions and the needs of vulnerable groups. Taken together, these sources support evaluating whole diets, affordability and life stage rather than prescribing one protein-source ratio for every population. They do not establish that any particular meat analogue is nutritionally interchangeable with the food it replaces. [12][13]

Andrews and colleagues’ ecological analysis links national protein supplies with age-specific survivorship across 101 countries over 1961–2018. It reports different associations at different ages. This is relevant to the distributional question but cannot establish what a particular individual should eat, or identify the causal effect of substituting a specific product. Country wealth, health systems and many other conditions complicate the interpretation of national associations. [14]

3.5 The forward baseline does not imply a uniform shift away from animals

The OECD–FAO 2026–2035 baseline anticipates increasing dietary diversification and a larger role for animal-source foods in middle-income settings, constrained progress in low-income settings, and broadly stable high-income diets. It is a conditional model baseline, not a survey of intention or an inevitable future. Its policy importance is that widespread novel-protein substitution must be demonstrated rather than assumed. [15]

The meat chapter anticipates substantial substitution among animal meats, particularly toward poultry, and locates about 76% of additional meat demand in middle-income countries. For an animal-welfare project, a move from one animal species to another is a distinct outcome from substitution toward non-animal foods. This paper does not convert meat tonnage into animals affected: doing so would require species-specific production and welfare assumptions. [16]

4. Adoption and substitution

The adoption evidence should be read against the macro baseline established elsewhere in this report: OECD-FAO 2026 treats meat demand and dietary diversification as conditional projections, not proof that novel proteins will be adopted or displace livestock products. Section 5.3.1 of the meat chapter is useful because it separates rising meat demand from substitution among animal meats, especially toward poultry. That distinction matters here. A consumer moving from beef to chicken, from chicken to a plant-based analogue, from meat to pulses, or from dairy cheese to precision-fermentation cheese is making a different substitution with different welfare, climate, nutrition, and market implications. [15][16]

The adoption literature reviewed here is mostly not a literature of observed displacement. It is a mixed literature of systematic reviews, stated-preference surveys, discrete-choice experiments, willingness-to-pay studies, and a smaller set of US scanner-data studies. The most defensible conclusion is therefore methodological before it is geographic: willingness to try, willingness to buy, willingness to buy regularly, willingness to pay a premium, choice-experiment market share, and observed purchases are not interchangeable outcomes. The stricter question is whether an alternative product repeatedly replaces animal-source protein under actual prices and ordinary meal conditions. Few studies answer that question directly. 4.1 What can be claimed across product pathways

A cautious cross-pathway claim is possible only at the level of relative stated acceptance within specific study sets. Onwezen and co-authors' systematic review found that pulses and plant-based alternatives tended to have higher acceptance in the reviewed literature than cultured meat and insects. Gomez-Luciano and co-authors similarly found plant-based proteins were preferred over cultured-meat and insect-based alternatives in their four-country sample. Lwin and co-authors found plant-based protein was most accepted and insect-based protein least accepted in Singapore. These sources support the narrow claim that plant-based options often perform better in stated-acceptance research. They do not support a universal global ranking of technologies, and they do not prove replacement of animal-source foods. [17][20][34]

Traditional pulses and soy foods should not be collapsed into modern analogues. The systematic review treats pulses as a separate alternative-protein category, and the Mumbai discrete-choice experiment included chana alongside conventional meat, plant-based meat, and cell-based meat. In that study, chana was preferred by one latent class, while other classes preferred conventional meat, plant-based meat, or cell-based meat. The inference for India is therefore bounded: in at least one urban Indian choice experiment, traditional pulses and newer substitutes appealed to different segments. That is not evidence that all Indian consumers will substitute pulses for meat, but it is enough to warn against treating plant-based burgers as the only plant-protein pathway. [17][26]

Fermentation pathways also need to be separated. Thomas and Bryant studied animal-free dairy cheese from precision fermentation across five countries and found substantial stated openness, while Kuhl and co-authors studied precision-fermentation cheese in Germany and found that information about genetically modified microorganisms slightly reduced willingness to try. Both studies concern dairy-like products, not biomass-fermented meat alternatives in general. Singapore's study includes mycoprotein alongside plant-based protein, cultured meat, and insect-based protein, but its findings should be read as Singapore-specific comparative acceptance, not as proof that mycoprotein will scale in other Asian or African markets. The safe claim is that consumers evaluate fermentation-derived products by product category, perceived quality, naturalness, trust, and benefit-risk framing; "fermentation" is not one acceptance category. [32][33]

4.2 Country contrasts without over-pooling

India appears promising in some stated-preference studies, but the scope is narrow. Bryant and co-authors' India sample was urban, educated, and higher-income relative to the national population. Arora, Brent, and Jaenicke studied Mumbai and found segmented preferences across chana, conventional meat, plant-based meat, and cell-based meat. The defensible country contrast is not that India is simply more accepting than the Global North. It is that some urban Indian samples contain multiple plausible adoption pathways, including traditional pulses and newer meat-like substitutes. Religion, diet, city, income, language, and food norms remain open sources of heterogeneity unless a study measures them directly. [19][26]

The retrieved Chinese studies suggest sensitivity to information framing rather than uniform enthusiasm for adoption. Chung, Bryant, and Asher found substantial reported prior trial of plant-based meat, but trial and purchase intent differed, and perceived healthiness was the strongest attitudinal predictor of purchase intent. Zhang, Li, and Bai found limited prior knowledge of cultured meat in three Chinese cities, with information provision reducing opposition. Chen, Zhou, and Hu found that positive nutrition and environmental information increased stated WTP for plant-based and cultured meat while negative information reduced it. These studies support a China-specific inference that health, nutrition, safety, knowledge, and information framing matter. They do not establish national repeat purchasing or meat displacement. [27][28][29]

Brazil appears in two cross-country sources with different product categories. In Gomez-Luciano and co-authors' meat-alternative study, Brazil was part of a four-country comparison in which plant-based options were preferred over cultured-meat and insect-based options, and Brazil's willingness to purchase cultured meat was lower than Spain's in the reported comparison. In Thomas and Bryant's animal-free dairy survey, Brazil showed high stated openness to animal-free dairy cheese. The study-specific reading is that Brazilian responses may vary by product pathway and framing. The sources do not justify a broad statement that Brazil is either resistant or highly receptive to alternative proteins overall. [20][32]

The retrieved South African choice experiment provides a country-specific observation; it cannot represent the continent. Tsvakirai and co-authors' South African choice experiment found market potential for plant-based and lab-cultured patties under the conditions of the experiment. Gbejewoh, Marais, and Erasmus review the wider sub-Saharan African literature and stress limited consumer research, socio-cultural meanings of meat, cost, health, and nutrient questions. The proper inference is an evidence gap with some South African stated-choice evidence, not a continental adoption forecast. [30][31]

Germany illustrates how environmental or animal-welfare benefits can coexist with technology-specific reservations. Weinrich, Strack, and Neugebauer found moderate cultured-meat acceptance, with ethical benefits and emotional objections pulling in opposite directions. Kuhl and co-authors found precision-fermentation cheese acceptance was influenced by quality, animal-welfare benefits, GMO information, farmer-livelihood concerns, and market-power concerns. This supports a bounded claim about German respondents in these studies: benefit arguments mattered, but naturalness, technology, and political-economy concerns also mattered. It does not support a claim about all German alternative-protein categories. [21][33]

Singapore is a special case. The Lwin and co-authors survey is valuable because it uses a nationally representative sample and compares plant-based protein, mycoprotein, cultured meat, and insect-based protein in one market. But Singapore's high income, food-import dependence, public food-security strategy, and regulatory visibility make it a poor proxy for "the Global South." Singapore is treated separately as an Asian institutional case, not as a general emerging-market adoption signal. [35][36] [34]

4.3 What the US scanner evidence does and does not prove

The retrieved evidence about actual purchasing comes primarily from US scanner studies, with limits imposed by product category and sales channel. Neuhofer and Lusk used IRI household scanner data from November 2018 to November 2020 and found that about 20% of included households purchased a plant-based meat alternative at least once, while most PBMA buyers also bought ground meat. They also report that ground-meat consumption did not clearly fall after a household's first PBMA purchase. Cuffey, Chenarides, Li, and Zhao used Nielsen Homescan data from 2014 to 2019 and found PBMA spending fell by more than 75% in months after the first purchase month, while meat spending did not decrease in that first purchase month. Zhao, Wang, Hu, and Zheng used Nielsen retail Scantrack data from January 2017 to July 2020 and found PBMA demand was still small relative to meat demand, with demand estimates indicating complementarity with beef and pork and substitution for chicken, turkey, and fish. [23][24][25]

The US evidence should not be generalized as "plant-based meat never displaces meat." It says something narrower and more useful: in these US scanner datasets and periods, observed purchase patterns do not support a simple one-for-one replacement story for ground meat or fresh meat categories. Possible explanations include novelty, dietary variety or purchases for different household members; the scanner data do not identify those motives. That means adoption metrics based on sales or first trial can overstate animal-protein displacement. These purchase studies motivate caution when interpreting stated-preference findings from other settings. [23][24][25]

4.4 Antithesis and proposed causal test

An antithesis is that alternatives could expand dietary variety while having limited effect on animal-product demand. US scanner findings motivate this hypothesis; they do not prove a future market trajectory or establish buyer motives. It is supported more indirectly for sub-Saharan Africa by review evidence that price, socio-cultural meat meanings, and limited consumer research remain barriers. It should not be stated as a proven global outcome. It is a risk hypothesis: where alternatives are expensive, urban, novel, or adopted by consumers already buying many protein products, category growth may coexist with continued animal-source consumption. [23][24][25][31]

Proposed research design: begin with a feasible retail or institutional food-service setting and test a defined intervention, such as a price or menu change, against a credible comparison. Measure repeat purchasing or selection and the incumbent food displaced. Choose either food-product weight or protein mass calculated from composition data, and keep retail weight, edible portion and protein mass distinct. Determine follow-up and sample size through the research question and design; an illustrative six-month follow-up is a proposal, not an established minimum. Cross-country replication would be a later extension, with harmonized outcomes and locally appropriate foods. Section 9 outlines bounded studies that could follow this review.

Inference for synthesis: the adoption literature supports a cautious framework rather than a global ranking. Traditional pulses and soy foods, plant-based analogues, precision-fermentation dairy, biomass fermentation, cultivated meat, and insects are not interchangeable. Each pathway faces a different combination of familiarity, taste, price, trust, perceived naturalness, religious or dietary fit, regulatory context, and meal-format fit. This paper therefore separates awareness, trial, purchase intent, repeat purchase, willingness to pay, and observed displacement whenever it compares Global North and Global South cases.

5. Talent and industrial capability

The eligible evidence reviewed here does not support a global ranking of alternative-protein talent hubs by workforce size. It supports a narrower claim: governments and universities can be assessed by the public capabilities they are building for the protein transition. Those capabilities include paid training, shared pilot infrastructure, regulatory science, technician pathways, translational research, and evidence systems that can show whether a programme has actually changed capacity. A city with visible companies or job postings is not automatically a talent hub; a public programme with verified training, facilities, and governance may be a capability node even when its labor-market effects are still unknown.

This section therefore treats “hub” as an analytical label, not a rank. Cultivated meat, fermentation-derived proteins, and plant-based proteins require different skill bundles. Cultivated meat draws on cell biology, serum-free media, scaffolds, bioreactors, food safety, techno-economic analysis, environmental assessment, and regulatory capability [37]. Fermentation work depends on strain selection, feedstock optimization, downstream processing, quality systems, and scale-up. Plant-based work depends on ingredient science, crop development, extrusion or other structuring technologies, sensory work, nutrition, procurement, and manufacturing. No source reviewed here shows that one geography has all of these capabilities at scale.

Analytical capability definition

This paper defines a protein-transition capability node as a documented public or university-linked site or programme that contributes to training, practical career pathways, shared infrastructure, institutional coordination or durable local research capacity. These five dimensions form a proposed analytical framework. A record can establish an intended programme contribution without establishing that the intended outcome has occurred.

Several official programmes fit parts of that definition. The UK Alternative Proteins Innovation and Knowledge Centre call includes skills and talent development, placements, secondments, technical-professional development, facility access, and collaborative programme funding [38]. The UK roadmap and NAPIC launch add evidence of a national effort to connect alternative-protein research, commercialization, industry collaboration, and policy engagement [39] [40]. The Netherlands’ National Growth Fund cellular-agriculture programme is a national education-research-scale-up model with public funding, intended education modules, and scale-up facilities for cultivated meat and precision fermentation [41]. Singapore’s sources show a food-security R&D model that combines alternative-protein process work, food safety, commercialization, and an international precision-fermentation partnership, while also acknowledging cost and consumer-acceptance headwinds [35] [36]. India’s DBT-BIRAC smart-protein call places plant-based, fermentation-derived, and cell-culture proteins inside a high-performance biomanufacturing policy architecture and distinguishes proof-of-concept research from laboratory-to-pilot or pre-commercial scale-up [42].

The U.S. evidence is more fragmented. Tufts is a verified education-and-research node because the USDA NIFA record links cultivated-meat research objectives with outreach, extension, and educational platforms for workforce development [43]. Davis is a verified candidate site through a USDA NIFA project record that identifies the UC Davis Cultivated Meat Consortium as a federally and state-supported initiative relevant to alternative-protein education and training [44]. North Carolina should be described with more caution: the EDA record verifies a broader life-sciences manufacturing workforce strategy, including community-college and inclusive training investments, but it does not establish a protein-specific public centre or an alternative-protein workforce count [45]. The 2026 U.S. stakeholder study is useful for sector-wide themes such as scale, financing, policy environment, workforce development, and shared infrastructure, but it is not a hub census [46].

Brazil, India, and South Africa should not be framed as weaker versions of Global North hubs. They are cases where the talent question intersects with affordability, feedstocks, incumbent livelihoods, regional equity, and local industrial policy. Brazil’s peer-reviewed evidence warns that alternative-protein transitions may create new jobs and better working conditions, while also creating risks for conventional producers and workers, especially where qualifications, prices, and consumer acceptance remain constraints [47]. Another Brazil study finds that human capital matters when financial resources and social capital are available, which cautions against treating training as sufficient on its own [48]. South African academic evidence assesses emerging food technologies against affordability, nutrition, safety, consumer acceptance, local production, and regulatory environment; cultured meat and seafood score poorly against several near-term criteria, while indigenous crops and food-waste valorization appear more near-term in that study [49]. South Africa’s DSTI-FAO roadmap process is broader than alternative proteins, but it verifies a public effort around agricultural STI, institutional coordination, women and youth, skills development, value chains, and inclusive bioeconomy goals [50].

Analytical mechanisms for talent policy

The attraction and retention mechanisms below are policy proposals inferred from the source base. The documented programmes illustrate their design; they do not demonstrate their effects.

First, paid conversion training can move people from adjacent sectors into protein-transition work. Relevant pools include bioprocess technicians, food technologists, process engineers, fermentation specialists, food safety officials, procurement evaluators, and agricultural-extension workers. UKRI’s IKC call is the source for this mechanism because it lists training, upskilling, placements, secondments, exchanges, research technical professionals, and continued professional development as eligible or expected activities [38]. A talent programme should therefore report who is being paid, what prior skills they bring, and whether training leads to retained roles.

Second, shared pilot infrastructure can turn research skill into manufacturing competence. The Netherlands’ programme names scale-up facilities as an intended output, and India’s call includes scale-up from validated research toward pilot or pre-commercial levels [41] [42]. For map and policy purposes, shared facilities should be distinguished from general laboratory capacity. The relevant question is whether learners, public researchers, small firms, and regulators can work with equipment and quality systems that resemble the next stage of production.

Third, regulatory and technician capacity should be treated as talent policy. Singapore’s official sources pair alternative-protein R&D with food safety and commercialisation; UKRI’s IKC call names technicians, technical professionals, project managers, and industrial-engagement staff in addition to academic researchers [38] [35] [36]. The inference is that public programmes should train food safety assessors, allergenicity and toxicology specialists, quality managers, lab and pilot-plant technicians, and public procurement staff, not only founders and principal investigators.

Fourth, international mobility should be evaluated as circulation rather than extraction. Singapore’s NUS-University of Illinois precision-fermentation partnership is evidence of a formal international research collaboration [36]. FAO’s agricultural innovation-systems work frames innovation as interaction among research, extension, advisory services, governments, private actors, farmer organizations, and consumers, and notes weak innovation capacity as a barrier in many low- and middle-income countries [51]. The policy hypothesis is that diaspora fellowships, visiting professorships, shared doctoral projects, and return grants should leave curriculum, equipment access, or institutional capability in both partner systems.

Fifth, South-South cooperation should be considered where countries share constraints around affordability, feedstock sovereignty, cold chains, farmer livelihoods, or regulatory capacity. This is an analytical proposal derived from the Brazil, India, and South Africa evidence, not a demonstrated programme effect. Brazil’s plant-based meat study highlights price, regulatory competition, domestic protein sources, sensory profile, and manufacturing capacity [52]. India’s smart-protein policy emphasizes crops, agro-by-products, fermentation, and scale-up [42]. South African evidence highlights indigenous crops, fermentation, food waste, cultured meat, regulatory gaps, affordability, and time horizon [49]. These themes suggest a research agenda that is not simply technology transfer from North to South.

What the evidence does not show

The current source base is useful for programme design and barrier mapping, but weak for outcome evaluation. It does not provide comparable vacancy counts across Boston, Davis, North Carolina, Wageningen, the UK, Denmark, Singapore, Brazil, India, or South Africa. It does not show whether public training programmes retain graduates after two or three years. It does not prove that shared facilities increase small-firm survival. It does not show that international partnerships prevent brain drain. The OECD-FAO outlook can provide macro agricultural context, but it should not be used to infer an alternative-protein workforce trajectory unless a specific section of the report directly supports that claim [53].

Policy evaluation must test what an intervention adds. A fellowship could bring in people who lacked a funded route, improve their competence, or support work that otherwise would not proceed. A shared pilot plant could enable previously inaccessible experiments. Regulatory-science training could improve the quality and consistency of assessment. These possible forms of additionality require counterfactual evidence; participation alone is insufficient.

Programme evaluations, graduate tracking, facility-access records and administrative workforce data would strengthen these conclusions. Until those are available, public commitments and reported activities establish the objects to evaluate, rather than a demonstrated return on training expenditure.

7. AI, technical progress and changing skill requirements

The claim that AI changes the protein transition is too broad to evaluate without a specific task and outcome. A defensible version asks whether a method changes the cost or time needed to reach a reproducible technical result, whether that result transfers to production, and whether the change alters a real allocation decision. This prevents a research-performance result from being treated as a finding about a factory, a labour market or consumer behaviour.

Cosenza and colleagues demonstrate multi-objective Bayesian optimization of serum-free media in an adapted C2C12 cell system. One medium achieved 23% more growth at 62.5% of the control’s cost. The discussion limits generalization to the adapted cells and emphasizes the need for re-optimization and biological understanding when moving to other cell types. This supports a role for experimentally grounded computational training; it does not establish a 37.5% reduction in the cost of cultivated meat. [3]

Engineering economics supplies the necessary counterweight. Humbird’s analysis investigates capital and process constraints in conceptual cell-culture facilities. Pasitka and colleagues combine experimental results with a techno-economic analysis of a theoretical 50,000-litre facility. The latter’s modeled facility is not equivalent to an audited commercial operation. These studies differ in systems and assumptions, so their estimates should not be plotted as a continuous industry learning curve. [4][60]

Analytical implication: universities should train people who can join computation to experimental design, process engineering and safety evidence. A narrow “AI talent” category would miss the practical work required to validate a prediction, maintain a reactor, reproduce a batch and translate an ingredient into an acceptable meal. Whether computational scientists are the binding shortage remains an empirical question. A useful diagnostic would compare project delays, vacancy duration, equipment access, assay quality and process failures within the same pathway and jurisdiction.

The arrows in the diagram are research questions. Each requires separate evidence. Better laboratory performance could coexist with unchanged total manufacturing cost. Lower manufacturing cost might not reduce retail prices. Lower prices might attract purchases that replace another plant food rather than meat. Even lower meat purchases may not translate immediately into lower production because inventories, exports and price responses intervene. The proposed framework therefore places checkpoints between each stage instead of assuming that the whole chain follows from the first success.

8. A public model for integration

8.1 A common problem statement

Policy proposal: organize collaboration around an explicit food-system problem, not a generic promise to become an innovation hub. A ministry and its university partners could specify a nutritionally adequate food, a target population, an affordability condition and a locally relevant supply route. The research consortium would then identify the skills and facilities needed to solve that problem. The same process can support pulses, fermented ingredients or cultivated products without deciding the winner in advance.

Public authorities should distinguish four functions: providing food and nutrition objectives; funding and evaluating research; assessing product safety; and purchasing food. Coordination is useful, but conflating these functions could compromise evaluation. A regulator’s participation in scientific discussion should not be portrayed as an assurance of future approval. A public procurement trial should measure acceptance, plate waste, cost and nutritional suitability rather than serving as a promotional event.

8.2 University integration

A practical curriculum would connect a common scientific foundation with pathway-specific rotations. Students in computational biology should work with experimental design and process data. Food-science students should work with sensory evaluation, local cuisines and nutritional assessment. Engineers and technicians should work with sanitation, maintenance, quality assurance and data integrity. Policy and regulatory students should learn how evidence dossiers, public purchasing and evaluation operate. These are proposed competencies; their relative labour-market scarcity requires dedicated evidence.

Universities should also count outcomes beyond graduate enrollment. Relevant measures include independently assessed practical competence, completion among underrepresented students, placement into appropriate work, retention, and the ability of partner institutions to run the methods themselves. Where an industrial placement is offered, its pay, supervision and intellectual-property terms should be explicit. An unpaid placement that excludes students with fewer resources would work against the stated capacity-building objective.

8.3 Government integration

Public funders can make training and facility access part of research design. For example, a pilot-facility award could require transparent booking rules, maintenance funding, technician positions and a documented route for university and small-enterprise users. An education award could require supervised practical work and follow-up of graduates. A research award could require reusable protocols and independent replication. None of these conditions proves programme effectiveness; they make effectiveness easier to test.

Talent attraction should be measured by the capacity gained, not only the people moved. Recruiting a senior scientist can be useful if that person builds a team, trains colleagues and creates durable experimental capability. It can be less additional if it simply transfers an existing team from a neighboring public institution. Retention deserves equal attention: technical career progression, stable support roles and operational budgets may determine whether a centre remains functional after a headline grant ends.

8.4 International integration and distribution

A North–South partnership should name what each institution retains. The proposed minimum is a negotiated division of experimental leadership, data stewardship, training places, authorship, equipment access and intellectual-property rights. A partner should be able to decline a project that does not answer a local food-system problem. Collaboration is not successful merely because samples or trainees cross a border.

South–South exchange can be organized around comparable practical questions, while avoiding assumptions that Southern countries have interchangeable institutions. Examples of partnership questions include ingredient processing under local input constraints, locally acceptable product formats, food-safety methods and shared training for technical staff. The case for cooperation should rest on documented complementary capabilities. The report’s hub map identifies places to investigate; it does not prove that every proposed pairing is already viable.

A capacity agreement should include continuation conditions. Can a partner maintain equipment, replenish inputs and employ trained staff? Are results accessible in languages and formats that local practitioners can use? Can an institution repeat the work after the visiting team leaves? These questions make “integration” observable. They also prevent training success from being defined exclusively by placement in Northern institutions.

9.1 Indicator design

Each indicator needs a unit, population, baseline, source, reporting interval and interpretation rule. “More jobs” is not adequate. A stronger measure is retention in relevant paid roles among a defined training cohort at twelve and twenty-four months, with the denominator and missing follow-up reported. “Better affordability” should specify the compared foods, serving or nutrient basis, location, date and household resources. “Research capacity” should include the ability to perform and reproduce work, not only publication totals.

A useful dashboard would record the following without compressing them into a single readiness score: product-to-incumbent price ratios; repeated purchase and substitution; reliable facility operating time; independently replicated process outcomes; time to complete safety evidence; paid training completion; placement and retention; and local control of research and infrastructure. The paper proposes these indicators because they connect decisions to outcomes. It does not claim that a complete international series currently exists.

9.2 A feasible empirical extension

The next original study should be smaller than the whole comparative survey. One option is a paired institutional study: select a Northern and a Southern university or public programme with accessible records, define one protein pathway, and compare how a specified constraint affects research completion and workforce development. Pair interviews with document analysis, training outcomes and facility-use evidence. Obtain the applicable ethics and data permissions before collecting personal information.

A second option is an adoption study nested in public food service. Use a preregistered design with a credible comparison, ordinary prices or clearly documented subsidy, repeated exposure, nutritional assessment and measures of what the meal replaces. Account for clustering, menu changes, attendance and spillovers. Sample size must follow an effect-size and power justification; this paper supplies no invented minimum. Such a study would strengthen the bridge between the consumption and talent workstreams because it identifies the skills actually needed to deliver an acceptable intervention.

A third option is a programme-level workforce study. Record competencies, paid placements, vacancies, retention and project outcomes with clear definitions. A vacancy that disappears has not necessarily been filled; a person attending a workshop has not necessarily acquired competence. Use aggregate reporting where possible. Compare funded participants or institutions with an appropriate counterfactual and document selection effects. The resulting evidence would be more informative than a map of visible employers alone.

11. Limitations and research agenda

Four limits materially affect interpretation. First, the numerical comparison is a purposive sample from a fixed publication. It is not a current census of global diets, and the retained source has an explicitly recorded regional-label inconsistency. Second, consumption, willingness and institutional evidence have different dates and units. They cannot be pooled into a common transition score without additional modeling and justification.

Third, the hub inventory measures documented capability rather than labour-market size or programme success. Funding announcements show intentions; award records show support; facilities show potential capacity; evaluated outcomes are a separate category. Some relevant activity is likely absent from a public-source inventory, especially where documentation is limited. Missing entries are therefore not evidence of zero capacity.

Fourth, this paper contains analytical recommendations but no new causal estimate of a programme’s effect, no original survey and no completed human peer review. Access-limited sources are identified in the bibliography. The policy agenda should be revised as comparable consumption studies, training outcomes, production evidence and locally led research become available. Source eligibility provides provenance; methodological evaluation and critical reading determine the strength of a claim.

The immediate research priorities are to validate the most consequential country comparisons against updated primary series, harmonize product and adoption definitions across selected studies, develop an evaluated workforce case, and test one complete link from public intervention to actual dietary substitution. Those steps would transform a broad research foundation into a contribution that can support defensible decisions.
