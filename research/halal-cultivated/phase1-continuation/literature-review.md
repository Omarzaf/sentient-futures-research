# Cultivated-meat literature: argument and source review

Alqurashi et al. (2026) is useful as a map of production-stage questions and research priorities. It does not establish a product's halal status, an adopted international standard, or agreement among the institutions it names. Its cited reviews contain materially different arguments about avian embryos, slaughter and serum transformation. Hamdan et al. (2018) remains **full-text access unresolved**: this run does not claim to have reviewed its full argument.

This is a bounded literature deliverable. The full Phase One goal of 143 requirements across 16 countries remains incomplete. No scholarly approval or certification determination is supplied here.

The [structured review](literature-review.json) contains all 36 argument nodes, table/figure readings, counterarguments and source-family dependencies. Agent readings were independently reconciled; human scholarly review remains pending.

The original [Alqurashi article](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods15081288) was read through all six sections, 36 direct body paragraphs, 94 reference entries, three complete tables and three diagrams. Tables 2 and 3 reside in the XML's floating-content group and are included in the map. All diagrams were checked visually in the [publisher PDF](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/foods/foods-15-01288/article_deploy/foods-15-01288.pdf), pages 3, 11 and 12. Reading the 94 reference entries is not equivalent to independently reading all 94 underlying works.

`S5.P3`, for example, means section 5's third direct body paragraph, with lists retained inside their owning paragraph. Every argument node in the JSON includes the exact section ID, paragraph locator and original reference IDs. Tables and figures retain their original XML IDs. This is a locator system for the retained edition, not an invented numbering claimed to appear in the publication.

## Argument and counterargument

**Section 1: rationale and method.** Environmental pressures and growing demand motivate alternatives (S1.P1). The illustrative production pathway in S1.P2 and Figure 1 runs from biopsy and stem-cell expansion through scaffolds or 3D printing to structured meat. It is a generic model, not a dossier for the actual GOOD Meat process. S1.P3 uses international regulatory developments to motivate a Muslim-market gap, but references 26, 28 and 29 include advocacy or corporate news. Its characterization of an FDA consultation as pre-market approval should not be copied into the evidence library. Reference 31 is an analysis of regulatory data, not an independent set of manufacturing assays.

S1.P4's halal-market and import-demand figures do not provide missing Phase Two baselines. Its 2020 Pakistan consumption figure cites a 2017 review [36]; the original statistic needs verification before reuse. Its broad language about absence of formal guidance also needs narrowing: lack of harmonization can coexist with the retained MUIS 2024 guidance and IIFA 2025 resolution. S1.P5–P6 expressly present an integrative, stage-based narrative perspective, rather than a systematic or exhaustive review. The authors' analytical contribution should be evaluated on that basis.

**Section 2: certification and institutional claims.** S2.P1–P2 outline certification, prohibited materials, slaughter and contamination controls. The categorical statement about trace contamination is too broad to use without an applicable authority and its transformation or dilution doctrine. The article itself later permits documenting an istihalah route in its proposed protocol (S5.P7), while its cited Kashim review develops a qualified transformation argument.

S2.P3's Saudi leadership discussion cites national-identity and healthspan literature [48–49], which cannot establish cultivated-meat religious jurisdiction. S2.P5 cites official SFDA and Saudi Halal Center general pages [50–51], but no specific cultivated-meat decision or certificate. Recognition of foreign certifiers, accreditation and issuing product certificates are distinct functions; the article's terminology is not sufficient to verify them.

The JAKIM/IFANCA activity claim in S2.P6 leads to Tieman and Habir's company halal-responsibility viewpoint [38]. The accessible primary indexed passage discusses company governance and harmonization. The full article could not be obtained, so citation fit remains unresolved; this run does not claim the unread remainder lacks relevant content. The preliminary-fatwa claim points to an academic paper [42], and the OIC harmonization sentence lacks an attached specific instrument. S2.P7's Saudi no-certificate/no-authorization assertion is not supported by a demonstrated current register search: its attached reference [52] is a smart-agriculture/Vision 2030 chapter. None of these statements establishes an exact GOOD Meat certificate.

**Section 3: technical stages and disputed doctrine.** S3.P1 distinguishes possible starter cells and immortalization routes. Those categories must be matched to a real line: a fibroblast taken from an embryo is not thereby an embryonic stem cell. S3.P2 usefully records competing positions on live biopsy, embryos and slaughter. Recording that debate is not resolving it. S3.P3–P4 describe medium and scaffold options and the religious difficulties associated with FBS and animal materials. A bovine source alone does not establish halal collagen or gelatin; procurement and processing remain relevant. A serum-free formulation likewise does not demonstrate animal-component-free history across cell banking, enzymes, carriers, scaffolds and processing aids.

The two full-text dependency sources expose significant counterpositions. In [Kashim et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sjbs.2022.103501), section `s0060`, paragraph 16, the authors conditionally discuss complete transformation of serum-grown cells, dependent on scholarly opinions. They explicitly contrast this with a washing requirement they attribute to Hamdan's earlier paper, and separately note MS1500's prohibition on blood, plasma and serum. Their conclusion (`s0065`, paragraph 1) is framed as their preliminary view. It is not a certificate or an official universal FBS permission. The text is retained in [original XML](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC9712126/fullTextXML) and a [locator map](literature-review.json).

In [Hamdan et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e28491), section `sec4.2`, paragraphs 6–7, the authors distinguish eggs from embryos extracted from a mammal's uterus. They then argue that cultured chicken produced from avian embryos can become pure through transformation. Paragraph 6 cites classical authorities; paragraph 7 is their analytical inference. This is a substantive counterargument to treating donor-bird slaughter as an already settled prerequisite, but it is not a verified consensus or a product determination. The [2024 original](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11004531/fullTextXML) is a different work from the required 2018 paper.

**Section 4: acceptance evidence.** Price, availability, trust, perceived naturalness and food neophobia can affect attitudes alongside religion (S4.P1–P4). Certification therefore does not establish uptake. Table 1 combines hypothetical willingness to try, acceptance, doubts, buying intentions, interest and qualitative conditions from unlike samples. A specific internal issue needs correction before reuse: the table's 56 percent figure is doubts in a Malaysian sample of 102, whereas S4.P3 describes a 17–56 percent acceptance range. The endpoints should be rechecked against the individual surveys; this review does not replace them with a newly invented pooled estimate. S4.P5 and the table caption explicitly warn about hypothetical intentions, nonrepresentative samples and lack of a systematic review or meta-analysis. Preserve those limitations.

**Section 5: proposals.** S5.P1 calls the recommendations directional and facilitative. The proposed governance arrangement in S5.P2 and Figure 3 cannot establish an existing OIC-led hierarchy, partnership or agency commitment. S5.P3 recommends primary cells from slaughtered animals and calls for avian-embryo clarification. Reference 54 is a technical biopsy source, not an issued religious ruling. The egg-transformation argument in the cited 2024 review should accompany this recommendation rather than disappear behind it.

S5.P4 recommends medium substitution and economic/scaling development; technological feasibility does not demonstrate complete halal compliance. S5.P5–P9 explicitly propose a certification framework, a Halal-by-Design protocol, traceable banks, ingredient documentation, segregation, audit, advisory boards, sandboxes and pilot collaborations. These are authors' proposed controls. Table 2 has six guideline rows without row-level references; Table 3 has five qualitative risk rows without validated probabilities or scores. Figure 2 organizes concerns and solutions but supplies no tested decision thresholds or certificate. Table 2's possibility of a non-slaughter route and S5.P7's exclusive slaughter-derived banking proposal should remain distinguishable.

**Section 6: limits and research order.** S6.P2's innovation benefits are hypotheses. S6.P3 explicitly acknowledges the absence of empirical data from halal-certified cultured-meat systems and dependence on evolving interpretations. That admission is central to the article's evidentiary ceiling. S6.P4's ordering of material and jurisprudential work before economic/adoption evaluation is the authors' research recommendation; it does not prohibit carefully labeled conditional scenarios. S6.P5 advocates engagement, without reporting a completed cross-authority agreement.

## Institutional claims and shared source families

The article directly cites no original MUIS or IIFA cultivated-meat ruling. Its general Saudi official links are a different kind of evidence. Academic reviews [41–42] are valuable accounts of debate but share earlier material, including the required Hamdan paper; counting each review as another independent ruling would exaggerate corroboration.

The 2024 Hamdan review's Table 2 mixes research papers, individual opinions, institutional material and journalism. Its 2013 Gulf News interview with Abdul Qahir Qamar [bib27] is an individual interview with an institutional affiliation, not an adopted IIFA resolution. Other press families include NST [bib39], Wired Middle East [bib40], Al Anba [bib46], Salam Gateway [bib47], Reuters Context/YouTube [bib49] and Bloomberg [bib50]. A later review repeating these accounts does not create a new originating decision.

There are more promising original-institution routes in that review: American Fiqh Academy's `res07` [bib34], Federal Territories Mufti's Irsyad 595 [bib43], an East Java NU decision reference [bib44], and an NU Online conference report [bib45]. This literature task read those citation entries, not the complete underlying instruments. Their verification belongs to the institutional dossier. Institution-hosted news still needs to be distinguished from the complete resolution it reports. The 2024 paper's conclusion calling for IIFA/JAKIM/MUIS collaboration is a recommendation, not proof of an existing joint body, and it predates IIFA Resolution 265.

The structured dependency map records these trails and their limits. No single-author or single-family claim has been promoted to an accepted, multiply corroborated institutional finding.

## Application to the documented GOOD Meat version

The comparator is the [FDA-hosted GOOD Meat dossier, CCC000001](https://www.fda.gov/media/166346/download), with its March 2023 consultation and later labeling correction. It describes a line derived from the torso of a 10-day chicken embryo: UMNSAH/DF-1 fibroblasts, ATCC CRL-12203, then C1F/C1F-P1 development. This is not the article's generic adult-biopsy/ESC/scaffold scenario. Religious analysis needs the precise procurement and developmental facts, not a generic cell label.

The US consultation version includes bovine-serum history and FBS in the disclosed manufacturing pathway. Confidential or incompletely itemized components remain unknown. The later Singapore serum-free announcement concerns a distinct version; it does not retrospectively erase serum use or establish that every bank, medium component and aid is animal-free.

The actual process grows suspension biomass and uses centrifugation and sodium-chloride washing. An introduced scaffold is not identified in that disclosed flow; this is narrower than proving that no animal-derived aid exists. Dossier Table 33, printed page 60, reports post-harvest BSA of 42.46, 11.97, 5.27 and 3 micrograms/g, with a reported mean of 15.67 micrograms/g. Washing therefore cannot simply be treated as a demonstrated zero-residue result. Nor can those assays decide the legal meaning of impurity, dilution or transformation. The harvested biomass composition is not necessarily a retail recipe.

FDA's no-questions consultation is not an all-version approval or a halal certificate. The literature supplies no certificate matched to the exact line, facility, formulation, process version and final product. The correct status is **exact matching certificate not established in this evidence set**, not an assertion that no certificate exists anywhere.

## MUIS/IIFA dispute remains held

The retained [MUIS release of 3 February 2024](https://www.muis.gov.sg/resources/media-releases/3-feb-24-fatwa-on-cultivated-meat/), especially paragraph 8, requires a permissible animal source, halal ingredients and a clean/non-toxic product. It does not expressly settle donor slaughter there. The retained official Annex C explanation discusses exceptions for cell extraction, but its concluding conditions require halal-slaughtered sources. The intended relationship between those passages and any supersession remains unresolved. This review does not choose a convenient passage and discard the other.

[IIFA Resolution 265 (10/26)](https://iifa-aifi.org/en/56085.html), Third(1)–(3), distinguishes living permissible animals and slaughter where required, excludes prohibited media and substances, and requires specialized oversight. Fourth–Sixth add disclosure, safety and a complementary role alongside conventional meat. The resolution's session was 4–8 May 2025; the retained webpage was posted 20 November 2025. Its institutional conditions are neither a named-product certificate nor a quantitative market cap. IIFA cannot resolve what MUIS intended on MUIS's behalf.

Alqurashi's framework can help formulate a precise scholarly question about the real chicken-embryo line and its serum history. Its authors' preferences cannot settle the retained institutional tension. Neither can the unread full argument of Hamdan 2018.

ResearchGate displayed only a publisher preview; no contact request was made. An unverified third-party upload surfaced in search and was not retrieved. Later papers were not substituted for this DOI.

Full-text review must still examine the precise donor/embryo distinctions, washing and transformation reasoning, underlying authorities, objections and qualifications. Kashim's description of its disagreement with Hamdan is a secondary attribution that identifies what to investigate; it is not a verified replacement for Hamdan's full argument. Two distinct scholarly/author routes did not resolve access, so repeated attempts were stopped.
