Protein consumption and alternative protein adoption in India and Pakistan

My central argument is that a protein transition becomes meaningful when a new product earns a recurring place in a household meal. A willingness to try it leaves open the harder questions: who can afford it, who trusts it, who decides to cook it, and what it replaces. I use India and Pakistan to examine how economic circumstances and social legitimacy shape those decisions.

The evidence makes national dietary stereotypes inadequate. India has substantial variation in animal-food consumption and an explicit public programme supporting smart-protein research. Pakistan has a formal halal certification infrastructure and marked differences in household food expenditure across consumption groups. These are different kinds of evidence, not exclusive national characteristics. Consumer studies reveal interest and concern, but cannot establish a national adoption ranking. Price, taste and access remain serious alternative explanations for differences that might otherwise be attributed to culture.

I recommend concentrating the next phase on plant-based meat alternatives in selected urban consumer groups, with cultivated meat and fermentation-derived proteins treated separately. The central outcome should be a change in routine consumption. This gives the team a focused comparative question and supplies the local evidence that its forecasting work needs.

Scope and the meaning of the evidence

This report consolidates secondary evidence reviewed through 14 September 2026 for discussion on 15 September. I have retained material that explains consumption, product acceptance or policy conditions in India and Pakistan. The earlier global surveys of investment, talent and AI applications remain background resources. They do not determine the argument here. I have not conducted interviews, tasting trials, a new consumer survey or a forecasting experiment for this report.

I distinguish familiar plant foods from products designed to substitute for animal foods. Pulses and chickpeas belong in the comparison as existing meal options; their consumption does not establish demand for a manufactured meat analogue. Cultivated meat uses cultured animal cells. Precision fermentation produces specified compounds through microorganisms, while mycoprotein uses fungal biomass. Acceptance of one process cannot be transferred automatically to another. The FAO and WHO distinctions between cell-based production and precision fermentation provide the starting terminology; the mycoprotein literature supplies a separate consumer evidence base.1

Measurement is equally consequential. FAO food balance sheets describe national food supply available for human consumption. They do not observe individual diets. Household surveys describe food consumption or expenditure at household level, sometimes converted into estimated nutrients. Food-frequency surveys record how often respondents report eating particular foods. Consumer experiments usually measure responses to hypothetical products. Each answers a different question.2

I consequently avoid a single India-Pakistan protein-intake league table. India’s nutrient estimates and Pakistan’s selected food quantities are useful within their respective survey designs, but placing them on one scale would manufacture comparability. Neither national averages nor willingness-to-buy percentages establish what a particular person eats or what a new product will displace.

What ordinary diets reveal

India’s official Household Consumption Expenditure Survey estimates unadjusted protein intake in 2023-24 at 61.8 grams per person per day in rural areas and 63.4 grams in urban areas. Cereals account for 45.9% and 38.7% respectively; eggs, fish and meat account for 12.4% and 14.1%. These are estimates derived from household food data, not individual dietary recalls. They also exclude dairy from the eggs-fish-meat grouping. I read them as evidence that the protein question reaches well beyond meat substitution.3

The social distribution is more revealing than the national label. In NFHS-5, 45.1% of women and 57.3% of men aged 15-49 reported eating fish, chicken or meat at least weekly. Among women, that measure varied from 40.7% for Hindus to 70.2% for Muslims and 4.3% for Jains. These are frequencies, not quantities or a measure of vegetarian identity. Nor does wealth produce a simple ladder toward meat: the corresponding figure was 44.0% in the lowest wealth quintile and 39.9% in the highest.4

Pakistan’s HIES 2024-25 reports monthly per-capita quantities of 6.59 kilograms of wheat or wheat flour, 0.26 kilograms of pulses, 6.15 litres of fresh or boiled milk, and 0.34 kilograms of chicken meat. These selected household-survey quantities make staples and dairy visible alongside meat; they do not establish individual protein intake or adequacy.5

In the lowest consumption quintile, wheat and wheat flour take 20.06% of food expenditure, compared with 7.04% in the highest. The corresponding mutton shares are 0.31% and 3.32%. Fresh and boiled milk remain substantial at both ends. These are food-budget shares rather than shares of total expenditure or protein.6

Hayat and colleagues provide a complementary demand analysis using HIES 2018-19. Their model classifies meat, milk and fruit as expenditure-elastic goods: demand increases more than proportionately with the food expenditure measure used in the model. The economic label “luxury” describes an estimated response, not a judgment that these foods are unnecessary. The study does not estimate demand for alternative proteins, and I do not transplant its coefficients into a forecast of novel products.7

Taken together, these findings change my starting question. I need to establish which purchases are constrained by cost, which express dietary commitments, and which perform a particular role in a meal. An expensive analogue could attract trial from a narrow group while remaining irrelevant to households seeking an affordable staple or dairy accompaniment. Conversely, limited meat purchases cannot by themselves tell me whether a household would prefer more meat if its circumstances changed. That distinction needs direct investigation.

This also makes the familiar pulse dish an essential comparison. A household already avoiding meat has existing ways to compose a meal; a manufactured analogue must offer a reason to change. For that household, convenience or a particular taste might matter more than resemblance to an animal product. For a meat-eating household, resemblance might be central. These possibilities lead to different expectations about whom a product serves and what it replaces. My inference is that the relevant competition must be established from the household’s actual options before I interpret interest in a new technology.

How food becomes acceptable

I treat culture as something to investigate through decisions. The question is whether religious or social legitimacy changes a choice once the product, price and meal are specified. Religious affiliation is a useful descriptive variable, but I would also ask what a participant avoids, why, whether the rule changes outside the home, and who decides what the household buys. The Indian food-frequency differences establish heterogeneity; they do not isolate religion from region, caste, income or other influences.

A plant-based chicken product illustrates the problem. For one participant, resemblance to meat could be its principal attraction. For another, the same resemblance could make it unsuitable despite its ingredients. A cultivated product introduces a different question about animal origin and production. These are competing hypotheses, not findings about all vegetarians or all meat eaters. A credible questionnaire must leave room for both responses and for indifference.

In Pakistan, halal has an institutional dimension as well as a personal one. The Pakistan Halal Authority describes a mandate to ascertain the halal status of products, processes and services. PSQCA lists general halal-food requirements and standards for certification bodies. These official sources establish an administrative infrastructure. They do not establish that a particular cultivated-meat or precision-fermented product has received a Pakistan-specific ruling or certification.8

I would therefore separate three questions: what consumers believe is permissible; what a relevant certification body would require for the particular product; and what food-safety authorities require for sale. A general assurance that a technology is “animal-free” cannot answer all three. Product composition, production inputs and the institution making the claim need to be specified before I interpret a response as acceptance or rejection.

My account also needs an economic rival. Price, taste and access may explain much of the apparent contrast attributed to culture. If consumers in comparable circumstances respond similarly once the product and purchase conditions are specified, I should revise the cultural explanation. The comparison becomes informative when it can distinguish these possibilities, including the possibility that within-country differences exceed the differences between selected Indian and Pakistani groups.

Urbanisation and diaspora exposure remain possible influences, rather than established findings of this review. I would record actual travel, family connections, media exposure and access to relevant shops instead of assigning “globalised tastes” to young urban respondents. Likewise, I would ask who shops and cooks without assuming that the respondent, household head and meal decision-maker are the same person.

What the consumer studies establish

The Indian evidence gives reasons to investigate receptivity, with substantial limits. Bryant and colleagues surveyed 1,024 Indian respondents online in English. In that sample, 62.8% described themselves as very or extremely likely to purchase plant-based meat, and 56.3% gave those responses for “clean meat,” the study’s term for cultivated meat. The sample was disproportionately urban, affluent and educated. These are hypothetical purchase intentions collected for a study published in 2019, not 2026 adoption rates.9

Arora, Brent and Jaenicke offer a different design: a face-to-face choice experiment in Mumbai, with 394 usable responses, comparing conventional meat, plant-based meat, cell-based meat and chana. Its value for this project is the inclusion of a familiar alternative and the identification of different preference groups. Its choices remain hypothetical, and one city cannot stand for India. I take the two Indian studies as support for investigating distinct consumer segments, without converting their results into a market-size estimate.10

Pakistan’s cultivated-meat evidence warrants greater caution. Ahsan, Uzair and Ali surveyed 413 respondents through an online and assisted questionnaire in 2021; 77.8% were men. The paper reports concerns about safety, taste, price and religious compatibility. Its sampling limits and inconsistencies in how acceptance is presented make a single headline percentage unsuitable for my comparison. I retain it as exploratory evidence about questions worth asking, rather than an estimate of Pakistani demand.11

Dean and colleagues’ multinational mycoprotein study includes 649 respondents from Pakistan, with a mean age of 23.6 years. Its main explanatory model pools countries. It therefore broadens the range of potentially relevant concerns without supplying a Pakistan-specific adoption coefficient. It is evidence about fungal protein and stated willingness; it cannot answer how Pakistani consumers would respond to precision-fermented dairy proteins.12

These studies cannot be combined into a bilateral acceptance gap. Recruitment, language, timing, product descriptions and outcome questions differ. A percentage labelled “acceptance” in one paper is not interchangeable with a purchase-likelihood category in another. What the studies show is interest accompanied by conditions. Establishing which conditions matter most requires comparable questions.

Even observed purchases require interpretation. Neuhofer and Lusk analysed US household scanner data for ground meat and related plant-based alternatives. Most buyers of the alternatives also bought meat, and the before-and-after comparison did not show a fall in ground-meat purchasing after the first alternative purchase. This observational result is not a causal estimate for South Asia. It does demonstrate why I need separate measures of trial, repeat buying and displacement.13

For the team’s broader transition question, I would follow the sequence from an available product to purchase, habitual use, changes in animal-food demand and, eventually, production response. A sale establishes only part of that sequence. A household could replace a meat dish, replace a pulse dish or add an occasional novelty. Those outcomes have different implications and should remain distinguishable throughout the research.

Policy as a condition of adoption

India’s policy activity is documented. The 2025 DBT-BIRAC smart-protein call under BioE3 covers fermentation-derived, plant-based and cell-culture-based proteins, with support extending from applied research toward pilot and pre-commercial development. A parliamentary answer published on 23 July 2026 reports nine supported projects in the smart-protein sector. This moves the evidence beyond an announcement, while remaining evidence of research and development support.14

I interpret that support as a possible influence on the products and production capabilities that become available. Its effects on consumer prices, availability and trust require separate evidence. A funded project does not establish a food-safety approval, successful commercial production, repeat purchase or displacement. I would track those later stages independently rather than allowing the programme’s stated ambition to stand in for its effects.

For Pakistan, the reviewed official sources establish halal institutions and standards, but I have not established a directly comparable smart-protein programme. That is all I can show from the sources reviewed. It cannot support the broader claim that Pakistan has “almost no policy,” because a complete comparison would also need relevant food regulation, biotechnology support, provincial activity and instruments operating under other names.

The useful policy question is whether public action changes the conditions of purchase: price, permission to sell, credible certification, availability or information. India’s documented programme could influence those conditions through research and production capability. Pakistan’s halal institutions provide an established setting for investigating certification and trust. These are different pathways to examine, and neither proves adoption. I would follow their effects on households rather than count programme names.

Implications for the comparative research and forecasting work

I propose one common research design with explicit product boundaries. The primary comparison would examine a plant-based chicken product against a conventional chicken option and a familiar pulse-based option in a specified meal. A cultivated-meat vignette would be analysed separately. Fermentation-derived dairy proteins would enter only when I have a clear product description and sufficient local evidence to ask meaningful questions about them.

I propose urban sites as a practical first stage for locating products and comparing purchase conditions; I would verify availability before selecting them. They would not represent either country. A small purposive pilot should include variation in food expenditure, dietary practice and responsibility for buying or preparing food. Its immediate purpose would be to test comprehension and uncover omitted considerations.

The comparison needs a specified meal, price per serving, declared protein content, preparation requirements and source of any safety or religious assurance. I would use locally reviewed wording and translations, with voluntary, justified questions about personal circumstances. Outcomes should distinguish tasting, choosing at a stated price, buying again and the food replaced. Where feasible, a later purchase diary could test repetition. A representative survey would require a separate sampling plan.

Questions that would change my interpretation

Proposed explanation What I would examine What would weaken it
Affordability limits adoption Choices at specified prices relative to familiar meals Persistent rejection when cost and access are comparable
Religious or social legitimacy changes choice Responses to product-specific information and credible assurance Little change after information, with taste or price explaining choices
Meal compatibility supports repeat use Preparation, household acceptance and the food replaced Trial without repeat use, or preference for familiar pulse dishes
Policy support improves access Documented progress from funded work to approvals, availability and price Research activity without observable changes in purchase conditions

These are proposed tests, not experimental results. Several mechanisms may operate together. A pilot can improve the questions and reveal contradictions; establishing independent effects would require a stronger design.

For the shared LLM questionnaire, my contribution is to make forecast questions answerable and locally specific. Each should name the product category, geography, outcome, denominator, deadline and resolution source. “Alternative proteins will grow in South Asia” is too broad to resolve. A retail-availability question, for example, would require a preselected retailer sample, a definition of availability and an observation date. If the team lacks those data, I would first treat it as a monitoring question rather than assign a probability to an undefined event.

The forecasting literature provides a reason to experiment carefully. Schoenegger and colleagues compared an ensemble of 12 LLMs with a human crowd on 31 binary questions. The aggregate beat a no-information benchmark and was statistically indistinguishable from the human crowd, but the authors also report poor calibration and a small question sample. This does not validate long-term protein-market forecasts or establish that a model panel represents Indian and Pakistani consumers.15

I would initially compare generic prompts with prompts containing the same verified local evidence, looking for changes in assumptions, cited reasons and unresolved gaps. That exercise could show whether local context changes model responses. It would not, by itself, demonstrate improved forecasting accuracy. Repeated model outputs would measure response variability; disagreement between models would not become a calibrated probability interval simply because it is easy to plot. Accuracy claims would require outcomes, a predefined scoring method and an appropriate benchmark.

My recommendation for the team

I recommend retaining India-Pakistan as a focused comparative case and making the household meal and purchase decision its organising units. The immediate task is to establish whether the same proposed substitute encounters different constraints among carefully described consumer groups. Plant-based meat offers the clearest starting point for that comparison; other production categories should retain their own definitions and evidence requirements.

For Tuesday, I would seek agreement on the primary product and meal, the sites and consumer groups that are feasible to study, and access to local reviewers or data. I would also agree that the first pilot is designed to improve the questions. A national demand estimate or calibrated forecast would be a later deliverable requiring additional evidence.

The contribution I can defend now is a comparative explanation with explicit tests. The reviewed evidence makes national stereotypes inadequate, identifies concrete economic and institutional differences, and shows why stated interest must be separated from routine consumption. The next phase should determine which of those differences actually changes a purchase and what that purchase replaces.

Research status and source preservation

I prepared this secondary synthesis with AI-assisted research, drafting and source checks; my final review is required before circulation. Descriptive findings are attributed to their sources. The comparison, interpretation and proposed tests are my analytical contribution. I have not independently reproduced the cited studies’ microdata analyses, established current national alternative-protein sales, or completed primary fieldwork.
